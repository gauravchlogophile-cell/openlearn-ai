Sometimes you do everything in the last seven lessons well and people still do not use
it. That is not a disaster. It is one of the most useful things that can happen to a
project, provided you can read it.

## What it tells you

Non-adoption is data, and it points in a few directions. Work through them in order.

### The problem was not theirs

The most common reason. L1 was about this: the tool solved a problem you identified and
the team did not feel. They were asked to adopt a solution to a difficulty they did not
have.

**Test:** ask people what the hardest part of their week is. If your tool does not touch
it, you have your answer.

### The first experience went badly

P4·L2's point: a person who gets a poor answer on the first attempt does not file a bug,
they decide the thing is not for them.

**Test:** ask what happened the first time they tried it. If a significant number describe
a bad first answer, the tool may be fine and the entrance wrong. Check what a new user
sees first, and whether the first request they would plausibly make works.

### It costs more than it saves

L6: the whole cost, including checking and rework. People often reach this verdict
quickly and correctly, and without saying so.

**Test:** time the task with and without. If the tool is slower in practice, they were right.

### There was no time, or no permission

People may want to use it and have no room. They are busy, nobody has told them they may
change how they work, or their manager expects things done the old way.

**Test:** ask whether they have been told they can spend time on it, and what happens if
the first attempts are slower than usual.

### Trust was damaged

A single visible mistake, a worry about monitoring (L5), or a sense that it was imposed.

**Test:** listen for stories. People repeat the one time it went wrong long after the
rest is forgotten.

### It is not for them

Some people do work for which the tool is not suited. That is a valid outcome, and it may
mean the use case is narrower than you hoped.

## Do not blame the users

It is tempting to conclude that people are resistant, lazy or afraid of change.

Even when that is partly true, it is a conclusion that ends the investigation. If
non-adoption is the users' fault, there is nothing to find. If it is information, there is
a great deal.

Ask yourself what you would have to believe for their behaviour to be reasonable. Usually
there is a version of that sentence you can complete.

## Decide: reshape, narrow, or withdraw

Once you know why, there are three honest choices.

**Reshape.** If the problem was real and the entrance was wrong, fix the entrance. Better
first-use examples, a clearer scope, an easier route in. Then pilot again.

**Narrow.** If it works for part of the team or part of the task, keep that. A tool used
well by four people for one job is a success, even if it was meant for forty.

**Withdraw.** If it solves nothing anyone has, or costs more than it saves, stop. Do so
cleanly, as P5·L8 described: turn it off, revoke access, remove it from the list, tell
people.

Withdrawing is not failure, and a project that can do it is more trustworthy than one that
cannot.

## Beware sunk cost

The effort already spent is not an argument for continuing.

You built it, trained people, wrote the page, ran the pilot. All of that is gone whether
you continue or not. The only question is whether continuing is worth the cost from here.

The pressure not to withdraw is strongest when you built the thing, which is exactly when
your judgement is least reliable. Ask someone who did not.

## Keep what you learned

A withdrawn rollout still produced valuable material.

> The problem as people described it, in their words.
> The test cases that exposed the tool's weaknesses.
> The objections, and which were right.
> An honest note on why it did not take.

Write it up briefly. Next time someone proposes the same idea, you will be able to say what
was tried and what happened, which is a far better position than starting again from
enthusiasm.

## Lrnon's retreats

The project has several examples of deliberately holding back, and they are worth reading
as decisions and not as setbacks.

Certification is built, with its assessments, rubric and verification page, and switched
off until two named reviewers exist and the guardian path has been tested with real
families. Community rooms are built and closed until a named safeguarding owner and deputy
exist. The assistant is open to staff only because its readiness gates are unmet.

In each case the thing works, and the project judged that the conditions around it were not
ready. None of that is a withdrawal exactly, and all of it is the discipline this lesson
describes: noticing that a technical success is not a readiness to roll out, and being
willing to say so.

It also corrected itself. The assistant's footer made a promise that could not be verified,
and was changed to say only what was true. That is the same instinct at a smaller scale.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

Think of a tool or process that did not take, in your organisation or elsewhere. Work
through the six causes above and write a sentence on each.

Then ask the sunk-cost question honestly: if it did not exist yet, and you knew what you
know now, would you build it?

## Recap

When a rollout does not take, treat it as information. Work through the causes: the problem
was not theirs, the first experience went badly, it cost more than it saved, there was no
time or permission, trust was damaged, or it is not for them. Do not blame the users.
Choose to reshape, narrow or withdraw, set aside sunk cost, and keep what you learned. Lrnon
has built several things and deliberately not switched them on, which is the same discipline
applied before launch.
