The launch is not the end of a rollout. It is the point at which the hardest part begins,
which is keeping the thing true and useful after the attention moves on.

The pattern from P5·L8 holds for people as much as for automations: what is not tended
decays.

## A place to ask

People will have questions, and where they put them decides whether they ask.

**One obvious place.** A named channel, an address, a person. Not four options, and not a
form that goes somewhere nobody reads.

**A named owner,** as P4·L5 argued for assistants. Someone whose job it is to answer, with
a backup, so that questions do not depend on one person's availability.

**A realistic promise about response.** *"Within a working day"* kept is better than
*"immediately"* broken. Lrnon tells people a person answers feedback within two working
days, which is a promise sized to the staffing that exists.

**No punishment for asking.** The questions that matter most are the embarrassing ones: how
do I get it to stop doing this, I pasted something I shouldn't have. If asking feels risky,
those never arrive.

## Failures are the gift

When the tool gets something wrong and someone tells you, that is the most valuable thing
that can happen after launch.

Each reported failure is a real input, from a real task, where the tool did not do what
was needed. It is a test case that cost you nothing.

**Make reporting easy.** One click or one sentence. A form with eleven fields is a form
nobody completes.

**Thank people,** visibly and promptly. The next person's willingness to report depends on
how this one was received.

**Close the loop.** Tell the person what happened to their report. *"We added this to the
instructions and it now handles it"* turns a complaint into a contribution, and tells
everyone else that reporting works.

**Put it in the test set.** P4·L6: an escape that is fixed but not recorded returns later.
Every reported failure should end up as a permanent case, with a note of what it broke.

## Keep what you wrote current

Several things from earlier lessons age, and each needs an owner and a date.

> **The instructions** of any assistant (P4·L5): version-controlled, with a change record.
>
> **The attached knowledge** (P4·L3): dated, and replaced when the underlying document
> changes.
>
> **The acceptable-use page** (L4): with a review date.
>
> **The training** (L3): the judgement parts age slowly; any tool-specific parts, quickly.
>
> **The test set:** re-run periodically, because the model underneath can change.

A line in the calendar for each. Quarterly is usually enough to start.

## Say what changed

When anything above changes, say so, where people can see it.

A short running log does it. *"12 Oct: tightened the instructions so replies no longer
promise refunds. 3 Oct: updated the returns policy document."* Two lines each.

It does three useful things. It tells people the thing is being looked after. It explains
changes in behaviour that would otherwise feel like the tool getting worse. And it creates
a record you can search when someone says *"it used to work"*.

Lrnon keeps a public record of this kind. The changelog is where content corrections and
new modules are listed, and the project's own rule is that a correction goes in a new
dated entry and not a quiet edit. This module's neighbours used exactly that: a wrong
count in earlier entries was corrected by a new entry, with the originals left as
published.

## Watch for quiet abandonment

The commonest end of a rollout is not a failure. It is a fade.

Usage drops. People go back to the old way without announcing it. Nobody complains,
because nobody is invested. A year later you discover it has not been used since spring.

The guard is to look. Check use and satisfaction at intervals, by asking people and by
looking at what is actually happening, and treat a decline as information to act on and not
as noise. L8 covers what to do when you find it.

## What a small team can do

None of this requires a department.

> One channel for questions.
> One person who owns the answers, with a backup.
> A one-line way to report a failure.
> A running log of changes.
> A quarterly look at the instructions, the knowledge, the rules and the test set.

That is an hour a month, and it is the difference between a rollout and a launch.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

For something you have rolled out, answer five questions:

1. Where does a person ask, and who answers?
2. How does a failure reach the person who can fix it?
3. Where are changes recorded?
4. When were the instructions, knowledge and rules last reviewed?
5. How would you know if people had quietly stopped using it?

Anything you cannot answer is the next job.

## Recap

After launch, give people one obvious place to ask, a named owner with a backup, a realistic
response promise, and no punishment for asking. Treat reported failures as free test cases:
make reporting a sentence long, thank people, close the loop and put each in the test set.
Keep instructions, knowledge, rules, training and tests current with owners and review dates,
and record each change in a short public log. Watch for quiet abandonment, which is how most
rollouts actually end.
