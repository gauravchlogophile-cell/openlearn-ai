A rollout meets resistance, and the way you read it decides what happens next.

The easy reading is that people are afraid of change and need persuading. Sometimes that is
true. More often, at least part of what they say is correct, and a plan that treats
objections as obstacles throws away the most useful information available.

## The objections, sorted

They fall into a few families, and each deserves a different response.

### "Will this take my job?"

The most important fear and the one most often handled badly.

Do not say *"no, of course not"* unless you control the answer and mean it. If you do not
know how the work will change, say so. If the tool is being introduced partly to reduce
cost, saying otherwise is a lie that will be found out, and everything you say after it is
discounted.

What you can honestly say is specific: what the tool is for, what it will not be used for,
what you do not yet know, and what you commit to. A concrete commitment such as *"this will
not be used to decide who stays"* is worth more than a general assurance, and only worth
making if it is true.

### "The quality will suffer."

Often correct. These tools produce fluent errors, and the people who do the work know
where the subtle mistakes live.

Treat it as expertise. Ask where they expect it to go wrong, and put those cases in your
test set (P4·L6). People who contribute the failure cases tend to become the ones who make
it work, because they have a stake in its being right.

### "What about our data and our customers?"

A privacy or confidentiality concern is a good sign: someone is thinking about the risks you
need thought about. P8 covers the substance. The response is a specific answer about what
goes where, under which plan, and with which safeguards, and not reassurance.

### "This isn't fair."

A fairness concern can be about who benefits, who carries the extra checking work, who is
measured against the tool, or whether it treats some customers or colleagues differently.
Take it seriously and ask what specifically seems unfair. It is frequently a real asymmetry
that you can fix.

### "I don't want to."

Sometimes it is plain preference, sometimes ethics, sometimes discomfort with how the
tools were built. These are legitimate positions, and an organisation can decide how much
room to give them. Being clear about what is optional and what is not is kinder than
leaving it ambiguous.

## Status is part of it

Tools shift who is valued. A person whose expertise was knowing where to find things may
find a tool does that. A person whose skill was drafting may find a first draft is now
free.

That is real, and ignoring it is a mistake. Name it. Ask what becomes more valuable, such
as judgement, knowledge of the customer, or checking, and give people a route to it. A
rollout that implies "the thing you were good at no longer matters" will meet resistance
that no training fixes.

## The monitoring question

Almost every tool can report who used it, how often and what for. The temptation to use
that is strong.

Be careful, because this is where trust is most easily destroyed.

**Do not track individuals in secret.** Usage reports quietly consulted in a performance
discussion, or a league table nobody agreed to, are discovered sooner or later, and the
discovery damages far more than the data was worth.

**If you measure, say so and say why.** Aggregate numbers to learn whether the tool is
helping are reasonable. Say that is what they are for, and keep to it.

**Never rank people by use.** High usage is not high value, and a low user may be
exercising exactly the judgement you want.

**Consider the law.** Monitoring people's activity can engage rules on workplace
surveillance and data protection (P8). That is a reason to be open, and to get advice
before collecting.

## Honest uncertainty beats confident reassurance

The strongest position available is also the plainest.

> *"I don't know exactly how this will change our work. Here is what I do know, here is
> what I'm going to find out, and here is how you can tell me what you're seeing."*

It is less comforting than a promise and more believed. People can tell the difference
between someone who is managing them and someone who is level with them.

## A note from Lrnon

Lrnon's guide for responding to challenge says to reply with the mechanism and not with
protest, and to look at a specific complaint the same day. The same attitude applies here.
When someone objects, ask which sentence, which case, which fear, and then look.

The project's own account of its limits, in its guides, is also a version of this. It says
it is run by one person, that certification is built and switched off, and that the
assistant is open to staff only. Not all of that is in the public-facing text, and some
sits in internal and funder guides, but none of it is flattering, and all of it is more
credible than a claim of completeness would be.

## Try it now (20 minutes)

List the objections you expect from your team, in their words. For each, write what you
would honestly say, including what you do not know.

Then check the draft for three things: any promise you do not control, any reassurance you
would not repeat if the facts changed, and any monitoring you have not mentioned.

## Recap

Objections to a rollout often have a real core, so sort them: job security, quality, data
and customers, fairness, and plain preference. Do not promise what you do not control, and
prefer honest uncertainty with specific commitments to confident reassurance. Notice that
tools shift status, and show people where value now lies. Never track individuals in secret
or rank them by use; if you measure, say so and say why. Ask what, specifically, someone is
worried about, and then look.
