P4 to P8 were about building something well. This module is about what happens when you
hand it to other people, which is a different problem and often a harder one.

The most useful sentence to start with is an uncomfortable one. P4·L8 said it of custom
assistants, and it applies to anything you roll out: **the honest base rate is that the
author uses it and nobody else does.**

## Why working is not enough

A tool that works has cleared the easiest hurdle. The harder ones are human.

**People already have a way.** It may be slow and clumsy, and it is theirs. They know
its failures and how to work around them. A new tool has to be better than that in
practice, not in a demonstration.

**The first experience decides.** P4·L2 made the point for assistants. A person who gets
a poor answer on the first try does not file a bug. They conclude it is not for them,
and that conclusion is very hard to reverse.

**Nobody has spare time to learn it.** Using a new tool costs attention now in exchange
for a saving later, and the person bearing the cost is often not the one who sees the
benefit.

**It was not their idea.** A change handed down by someone who did not ask them is
received differently from one they requested, even if the tool is identical.

None of this is cynical. It is how reasonable people behave when handed something new
during a busy week.

## Start with the work

Before proposing anything, find out what the work actually is.

**Watch it.** Sit with someone doing the task, or ask them to share their screen. Do not
interrupt to suggest improvements. Notice where they pause, copy and paste between
windows, or check something twice.

**Ask for a recent example.** *"Walk me through the last time you did this"* produces
real detail. *"Would you find AI useful for this?"* produces a polite guess.

**Ask what goes wrong.** Where do mistakes happen? What do you dread? What gets left
until Friday?

**Ask what they would not want touched.** Every team has parts of the job that carry
meaning, judgement or pride. A tool that automates those, however efficiently, will be
resented.

You are looking for a problem the person already has and would be glad to lose. That is
a much better foundation than a capability looking for a use.

## The problem named is rarely the problem

Expect a gap between what people say and what is true.

Someone says reports take too long. Watching shows the writing takes twenty minutes and
the rest is chasing three people for numbers. A tool that writes faster saves nothing
that matters.

Someone says they need help with email. Watching shows the difficulty is deciding what
to answer first, and a drafting tool would give them more email to send.

The way through is to keep asking *"and what happens next?"* until you reach a step that
someone would pay to avoid. That step, and not the one first mentioned, is the candidate.

## Check it is worth automating at all

Before building, run the checks from earlier modules on the candidate.

> Does the path genuinely vary, or is it a fixed chain? (P6·L1)
> Could a document, template or script do it? (P4·L8)
> Is it frequent enough to repay the keeping? (P7·L1)
> What data would it touch? (P8·L1)

If the candidate fails them, you have saved a build. If it passes, you have a better
brief than most tools start with.

## A caution from Lrnon

The project's assistant is the cleanest example available. It was built, it works, and
its purpose is sound. Its readiness gates require two hundred answers to be reviewed by
hand, and when this was written the review buttons had recorded none.

That is not a failure of the tool. It is the same gap in miniature: the mechanism exists
and the habit of using it does not. Building the means to give feedback did not by itself
create anyone who gives it, and the person who built it is the one person who knows
exactly where the buttons are.

## Try it now (20 minutes)

Pick a task someone else does and ask them for a recent example. Take notes without
suggesting anything. Then ask:

1. What took the longest, and why?
2. What would you most like never to do again?
3. What would you not want automated?

Write one sentence describing their real problem. Compare it to what you had assumed
before the conversation.

## Recap

A working tool is not an adopted one, because people have their own ways, the first
experience decides, and learning costs attention now for a saving later. Start from the
work: watch it, ask for a recent example, and ask what goes wrong and what they would not
want touched. The problem first named is rarely the real one, so keep asking what happens
next. Then check the candidate against the earlier modules before building. Lrnon's own
assistant shows the gap in miniature, with a review mechanism built and nobody yet using
it.
