At the end of a pilot someone will ask: *did it work?* The honest answer depends on whether
you decided in advance what working meant (L2) and measured it fairly.

Most rollouts measure the wrong things, because the right ones are harder to collect and
less flattering.

## Start with a baseline

You cannot show improvement without knowing where you started.

Before the pilot, measure the task as it is done now. How long it takes, how often it is
done again, what the error rate is, how satisfied the people doing it are. Roughly will do,
as long as you measure it the same way afterwards.

Without a baseline, you have testimonials. They are not worthless, and they are not evidence.

## Count the whole cost

A tool saves the time it takes to produce a draft. It costs the time it takes to use.

> **Writing the request:** often longer than expected for a good result.
> **Reading the output:** all of it, carefully.
> **Checking it:** P6·L7's point that the report is not the work applies to every output.
> **Fixing what was wrong:** sometimes more than starting fresh.
> **Dealing with the consequences** of an error that got through.

A tool that produces a draft in one minute and needs fifteen to verify has not saved
fourteen. It may have saved some, or it may have cost some. You only find out by counting
the whole of the work.

## Be sceptical of self-reported savings

Asking people how much time they saved produces a number, and it is not reliable.

People overestimate savings from something new and exciting. They remember the one spectacular
success and forget the three attempts that went nowhere. They are also reluctant to tell the
person who built the thing that it did not help.

Better evidence is observed rather than recalled: timings taken during the work, the number
of rounds of correction a document needed, error rates found in review. Where you must ask,
ask about specific recent tasks and not general impressions, and ask neutrally.

## Measure quality, not just speed

A faster process that produces worse work is not an improvement.

Decide how you will judge quality. Review a sample of outputs against a standard. Count the
errors that reached the next stage. Ask the recipients, if there are any, whether they
noticed a change.

The P4·L6 test set is part of this, and P3·L7's point is relevant: a result that passed
once is one sample. Look at enough cases to see the spread.

## Metrics that look like evidence

Some numbers are easy to collect and nearly meaningless.

**Logins and active users.** A tool people are told to use will be used. Activity measures
compliance.

**Number of prompts or queries.** More could mean more value, or more struggle.

**Satisfaction surveys alone.** People report liking things that save them nothing, and
dislike things that help.

**Anecdotes from enthusiasts.** The most motivated people produce the best stories, and are
not typical.

These are not useless as context. They are dangerous as proof, because they flatter.

## Decide what failure looks like

Back to L2: write down in advance the result that would make you say it did not work.

This is the hardest part and the most important. If the only outcomes you will accept are
good, the exercise is a ceremony. A real test can fail, and you should be able to say what
failing would look like before you look.

If the pilot shows no clear benefit, that is a result, and a useful one. It saved the
organisation from rolling out something that did not help.

## A gate that cannot change is not a gate

Lrnon's assistant offers a worked example, because the project got this wrong first.

The readiness gates in the admin console, for an assistant to be opened more widely, were a
hard-coded list with three of the four permanently false. They could not become true.
No amount of real review would have moved them. A gate that cannot change is a decoration,
and a measure that cannot move cannot tell you whether anything helped.

They are now computed from recorded reviews: the count of answers reviewed, and the rate
judged wrong, with the rate shown as *unknown* and not zero when there are no reviews. That
last detail is the baseline lesson in a different form: a rate of zero from no data would
read as "nothing is wrong" when it means "nothing is known".

When this was written those gates stood at zero reviews, because the part that needs a
person's time had not yet happened. The measurement can now move. Whether it will is a
matter of whether anyone reviews.

## Report it honestly

When you write up the result, put the unflattering parts in.

State the baseline and how it was measured. Report the whole cost, including checking. Say
how many people, for how long, and what the sample could not show. If the benefit was
modest, say it was modest. If it did not help, say that.

Honest reporting is also what makes the next proposal believable. A team that sees you
report a disappointing result will trust the next good one.

## Try it now (20 minutes)

For a use case you are considering, write the measurement plan. Include the baseline, the
whole-cost list, the quality check, and the failure criterion.

Then ask: if the result came back as "no clear benefit", would I report it, and would the
organisation let me? If not, that is the thing to fix first.

## Recap

Take a baseline before the pilot, because without one you have testimonials. Count the whole
cost: writing the request, reading, checking, fixing and the consequences of errors. Distrust
self-reported savings, measure quality as well as speed, and avoid logins, query counts and
enthusiasts' anecdotes as proof. Decide in advance what failure looks like and report
honestly, including a disappointing result. Lrnon's gates were once unable to change, and now
compute from reviews and show unknown rather than zero when there is no data.
