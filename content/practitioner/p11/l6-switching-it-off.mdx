This module's tagline promises "how to switch things off". Earlier modules covered the
pieces; this lesson puts them in order, because stopping is a design problem as much as
starting is.

Every automation will be turned off one day: in an emergency, because it is no longer
needed, or because something better replaces it. Whether that day is calm or frantic depends
on what you built beforehand.

## Two kinds of stopping

**Stopping in an emergency.** Something is going wrong right now. The need is speed and
certainty: make it stop, immediately, without waiting for anyone.

**Retiring on purpose.** The automation has done its job. The need is thoroughness: leave
nothing running, nothing connected and nothing forgotten.

They are different tasks and need different preparation.

## The emergency stop

P3·L8 gave four properties, P5·L7 tested them, and P6·L6 applied them to agents. Together:

> **One control.** Not "revoke the key, redeploy and clear the cache".
>
> **No deployment.** A build in the path means minutes you do not have.
>
> **Operable by whoever is there,** not only the builder.
>
> **Tested while the system is working.**

The fourth is the one that gets skipped. A switch tried only against a system that was
already broken will report success either way, because "stopped" and "already broken" look
identical. The observation that means something is: running, press it, confirm it stops.

Lrnon's own switch for the assistant is a worked example, with the honest history. For a time
it recorded a log row and changed nothing, while the console said the assistant was off. It
was then rebuilt so that the stricter of the deployed setting and the recorded stage applies.
Until 5 September every press had been made while the assistant was already failing for other
reasons. It was tested properly that day, with the assistant answering, and the log shows the
stop and the restore three minutes apart.

## Stopping must not strand the work

Pressing the switch is the easy part. The question that matters is what happens next.

If the automation was doing a job, that job still needs doing. Stopping it without a way to
carry on turns a malfunction into an outage.

So the stop control comes with a **manual path**: a written procedure, a person who can run
it, and a message to the people affected that says what to do instead.

Lrnon's assistant shows the shape. When it is off, whether by the switch, a cap or a failure,
a learner is told it is off, that search still works, and that a person answers feedback
within two working days. L3 made the point that this only counts while the person can really
answer.

Plan the **degraded mode** deliberately. Between fully on and fully off there are often useful
middle states: read-only, staff-only, a reduced feature set, drafts without sending. The
assistant's rollout has exactly this: off, staff, a slice of learners, everyone. Stepping down
a rung is often better than switching everything off.

## Who may press it

The asymmetry from P5·L6 applies: make the dangerous direction hard and the safe direction
instant.

Widening an automation should need care and sign-off. Stopping it should need nobody's
permission, because a stop that requires approval is not a stop. Lrnon's console says this in
as many words: one click, no approval, for turning the assistant off.

Be honest about the corollary. Anyone who can stop the automation can deny the service, so
decide who has the control, and keep a record of who pressed it and when. The rollout log
here records every change with the person responsible.

## Retiring one properly

When an automation has finished its life, the tidy-up is more than switching it off.

1. **Disable the trigger,** so that nothing starts it.
2. **Revoke its connections at the service itself,** not only in the platform. A disabled
   workflow with a live credential is a standing grant waiting to be misused.
3. **Remove it from the inventory** with a note of when and why (P5·L8).
4. **Delete or archive its credentials and its data** according to your retention rules (P8·L5).
5. **Tell the people who relied on it,** and say what replaces it.
6. **Keep what you learned:** the problem, the test cases, what worked and what did not.

Do not leave it disabled but present. A dormant automation with access is a risk that tends
to be rediscovered at the worst moment.

## Keep the manual path warm

L3 argued that skill fades. It matters most at this point, because the moment you press the
switch is the moment the manual path becomes essential.

A stop control with no usable manual path behind it is like a fire door that opens onto a wall.
Rehearse the fallback as well as the switch, together, at least once a year.

## Try it now (20 minutes)

For one automation you rely on, write down:

1. **The stop control,** where it is, and who may press it.
2. **When you last pressed it while the system was running.** If never, plan that test.
3. **What people do while it is off,** in a sentence they could follow.
4. **The retirement checklist** from above, with the services it would need to be revoked from.

Then run the stop test, with the people who would be affected told in advance. Time it.

## Recap

Plan to stop an automation before you start it. For an emergency, one control that needs no
deployment, can be pressed by whoever is present, and has been tested while the system was
running. Pair it with a manual path and a message to affected people, and design useful
middle states between on and off. Let stopping be instant and widening be careful. Retire on
purpose by disabling the trigger, revoking connections at the service, updating the inventory,
removing credentials and telling those who relied on it, and rehearse the fallback with the
switch.
