The hardest moment in a project is not building the thing. It is looking at a finished,
working feature and deciding not to launch it yet.

The pressure runs the other way. It works. People are waiting. The time has been spent. A
flag is one line away from "on".

This lesson is about the discipline of restraint, and about making it stick.

## Working is not ready

A feature can be technically complete and not ready.

**The people are not there.** Nobody to moderate, review, answer or be on call.

**The safeguards are untested.** A path for guardians or for reports exists in code and has
never been used with real families.

**The measurements have not happened.** The quality gates cannot be met, because the review
that would meet them has not been done.

**The wrong people could be hurt.** And the cost of discovering that by launching is paid by
them, not by you.

In each case, the code is not the thing that is missing. The missing piece is around it.

## Write the conditions down

The first discipline is to say, in advance, what must be true before it opens.

> Two named reviewers, who have agreed on a stated number of sample answers.
> A named owner and a deputy.
> A path tested with real examples.
> A quality threshold, measured.

Write them while you are calm and before the pressure arrives. A condition written after the
fact bends to fit what is convenient; one written beforehand does not.

Make them specific enough to be checked. "When we are confident" is not a condition. "When two
different reviewers have each recorded agreement on twenty sample answers" is.

## Where to enforce them

This is where most projects stop, and it is the important part. A condition that lives in a
document, or only in an interface, can be bypassed by someone in a hurry.

**Conjunctive, in the database.** Lrnon's certification shows the strong form. The function
that decides whether certification may open for a module is true only if the issuing switch is
not off *and* two named reviewers exist *and* they are different people *and* the rubric is
published *and* a sample agreement has been recorded *and* the guardian path has been tested
*and* the copy has been corrected. Its own comment says that a boolean flipped in isolation
opens nothing.

Every one of those must hold. Remove any and it stays shut. That is a gate the person in a
hurry cannot talk their way through, because the answer is computed from facts.

Learner rooms work the same way. The function that says whether they are open is true only if
they are switched on *and* a safeguarding owner exists *and* a deputy exists. Without both
people, nothing opens however the switch is set.

## Where the project falls short

Honesty requires the third case, which is the contrast.

Widening the assistant to everyone is meant to be locked behind four readiness gates and to
need a second owner's approval. In the console, the button is disabled until the gates are met.

The function behind the button, however, checks only that the caller is the owner. It does not
look at the gates and does not look for a second approver. The conditions exist in the page
and nowhere else.

So there are two kinds of lock in the same project. Two are enforced in the database and cannot
be flipped past. One is enforced by the interface and is a hint. The risk today is small,
because the only person able to call it is the person who would be the approver. The lesson is
the general one: a safeguard is only as real as the layer that enforces it.

The straightforward fix is to compute the same conditions in the database function and refuse
otherwise, as the other two do. It has not been done, and it is a decision for the owner,
because it changes who can do what.

## Hold the line socially, too

Technical enforcement helps with a person in a hurry. It does not help with a person in a
meeting.

**Name the owner of the decision.** Someone who can say "not yet" and is accountable for it.

**Publish the conditions.** When the criteria are visible, "not yet" is a statement of fact
about the criteria and not an opinion of the owner.

**Say what would change the answer.** Not "later" but "when these three things are true".

**Accept the cost.** Holding back has a price: delay, disappointment, something others could
have used. Say that it is a price and not pretend it is free.

## Restraint is a product decision

None of this is timidity. It is a view about where the risk lies.

For features that touch children, money, reputation or safety, the cost of launching too early
is paid by other people and is hard to reverse. The cost of waiting is paid by you, in time.
When the first is larger, waiting is the sound decision, and a project that can make it
repeatedly is more trustworthy than one that cannot.

It also keeps the useful thing: what was built is not wasted. It is ready for the day the
conditions are met, with the work already done.

## Try it now (20 minutes)

Pick something you have built and not launched, or something you are tempted to launch. Write
its opening conditions as a list of checkable facts.

Then mark each: enforced by the system, enforced by the interface, or enforced by nothing but
intention. Move one from the third or second group to the first.

## Recap

A finished feature is not a ready one: people, safeguards, measurements and consent can all be
missing while the code works. Write the opening conditions in advance as checkable facts, and
enforce them in a layer that cannot be bypassed. Lrnon's certification and learner rooms open
only when several conditions hold at once, in the database, while widening the assistant is
enforced only by a disabled button, an honest gap the owner has yet to close. Hold the line
socially too: name an owner, publish the conditions, and accept the cost of waiting.
