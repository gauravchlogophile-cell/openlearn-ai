The usual assessment of an automation is from the operator's side: cost saved, time saved,
errors reduced. There is a second ledger that is rarely written.

On the other side of every automated interaction is a person. The automation saves you
effort. What does it cost them?

## Ask from where they stand

Take a customer, a learner, an applicant or a patient. Walk through the interaction as they
experience it.

**Do they know a machine is involved?** Many people assume a person wrote the message or made
the call. Finding out later feels like deception, and often is.

**Can they reach a person?** If the only route is another loop through the automation, they
are stuck.

**Did they have a choice?** Opting in, opting out, or choosing a different route.

**What does it ask of them?** An automated process often moves work to the person: filling in
forms, repeating information, working out how to get the system to understand them. Efficiency
for you can be effort for them.

**What happens if it gets them wrong?** And how easy is it to say so?

This is a different question from whether the output is good. It can be good and still be
done to people in a way they would object to.

## The comfort test

A simple test captures most of it.

> **Would they be comfortable knowing exactly what is happening?**

If a customer read the full description of how their message was handled, would they be
unbothered, or would they feel misled? If a learner knew what the assistant did with their
question, would that be acceptable?

If the answer is no, you have two options. Change what you do. Or tell them and see what
they say. What you should not do is carry on in the hope that they will not find out,
because they usually do, and the discovery costs far more than the honesty would have.

## Disclosure

Tell people, plainly, at the point where it matters.

> That a machine is involved.
> What it does, in a sentence.
> What it was not told, or what it is not for.
> How to reach a person.

Keep it short and where it will be seen. A statement in terms that nobody reads does not
count. The test is whether a person in the moment knows what they are dealing with.

This connects to earlier lessons. Explorer E8·L2 covers the ethics of disclosing that you
used a tool. P8·L1 covered what goes where. P9·L5 covered being honest with your own team.
This is the same principle extended to the public.

## Offer a human

Wherever the stakes justify it, make a person available.

This does not have to be instant or universal. It has to be real and findable. *"If you would
rather speak to someone, here is how"* costs little to provide and changes how the whole
interaction feels.

For some groups it is not optional. Some people cannot use the automated route: no connection,
no confidence, a disability, a language the system handles badly (Explorer E10). An automation
without a human alternative excludes them. That is a cost you impose without seeing it.

## When people cannot walk away

The duty of care rises with how little choice the person has.

Someone choosing between two shops can leave. A patient waiting for a decision, an applicant
for a benefit, a child at school, a person dependent on a service, cannot easily.

For those, the usual defences of consumer choice do not apply. The only protection is what you
build in: disclosure, a human route, and restraint about what you automate in the first place
(L1).

Children are the clearest case, and P8·L6 set out how both laws treat them.

## Lrnon's version

The assistant tells people what it is. The footer states that the question is sent to an
outside AI service and asks people not to type personal details. When it cannot or will not
answer, it says so and points to search and a person.

It declines to give medical, legal, financial or personal-safety advice, and says that needs
a person. That is a deliberate refusal to occupy a role where a person on the receiving end
might be harmed by trusting it.

The platform's rooms for learners are kept shut until a named safeguarding owner and a deputy
exist, and the design has no private messaging at all. The reasoning is the one in this
lesson: for children, the project does not rely on their ability to walk away from a problem.

The honest limits: the assistant is open to staff only for now, and what it has been asked
to do is much smaller than what the lesson describes, so these choices have not been tested
at scale.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

Pick an automation that reaches other people. Write out the interaction from their side,
step by step, including what they are told.

Then apply the comfort test. If you hesitated, write what you would change or what you would
disclose. If you did not, show it to someone outside the project and watch their face.

## Recap

Judge an automation from the position of the person it is done to: do they know a machine is
involved, can they reach a person, did they have a choice, and what does it ask of them.
Apply the comfort test, whether they would be unbothered knowing exactly what is happening.
Disclose plainly at the point that matters, offer a real human route, and raise your care
where people cannot easily walk away. Lrnon's assistant says what it is, declines advice that
needs a person, and keeps learner spaces closed until named safeguarding people exist.
