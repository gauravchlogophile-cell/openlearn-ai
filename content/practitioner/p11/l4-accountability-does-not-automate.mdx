There is a sentence that should be retired from every organisation that uses automation:
*"I'm sorry, the system says no."*

It sounds like an explanation. It is an abdication. A system has no standing to be asked a
question, held responsible or put right. A person does, and the person is the one who
decided to build it and rely on it.

## What responsibility means here

When something goes wrong because of an automated step, a customer is wrongly refused, a
learner is misdirected, a record is corrupted, somebody has to be able to do four things:

> **Explain** what happened and why.
> **Reconsider** it, with a real ability to change the outcome.
> **Put it right** for the person affected.
> **Learn** from it, so it happens less.

None of these can be done by a system. So each needs a named person to do it.

## Who is accountable

Decide before launch, in writing:

**One accountable person** for each automation, with a backup. Not a team and not a vendor.
P4·L5 made this argument for a shared assistant and it holds for any automated process.

**Their authority matches their responsibility.** Someone held accountable who cannot
change the process, or override its output, is a scapegoat in waiting.

**The users of the tool remain accountable for what they send on.** P9·L4 put it plainly:
the person who uses the tool and sends the result is answerable for it.

## A route to a human

The person affected needs to be able to reach someone.

**Say clearly how.** In the message that tells them the outcome, not in a policy page. *"If
you think this is wrong, reply to this message and a person will look at it."*

**Make sure a person looks.** An appeal route that goes back to the same automation is a
loop and not a route.

**Give it a realistic timescale** and meet it.

**Make reconsidering genuine.** A human who can only confirm the system's output is a
rubber stamp. They need the information and the authority to disagree.

The more serious the consequence for the person, the more this matters. For decisions
that affect someone's money, access, education, employment or safety, it is the central
safeguard.

## The law has a view

Both laws from P8 touch this. The GDPR, in Article 22, gives people a right not to be
subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that produces legal effects or
similarly significantly affects them, with exceptions and safeguards, including the ability
to obtain human intervention and contest the decision. Whether a particular decision falls
within it, and whether an exception applies, are questions for advice on your facts.

The point for design is simpler than the legal analysis. If your automation makes decisions
about people that matter to them, assume you owe them a human who can reconsider, and build
that in from the start. It is the right thing to do, and it is much cheaper than retrofitting.

## A readable record

For a person to explain a decision, the decision has to be reconstructable.

P6·L7 covered the trace of an agent's run: every tool call and result, written by your own
code, kept long enough. The same applies to any automated decision. Record:

- What input it received.
- What it did, and which rules or model output it relied on.
- What it decided.
- When, and which version of the automation.

Then a person asked to explain can do so from a record and not from memory or guesswork.

Note the privacy interaction. That record may itself contain personal data. P8 covers how
to hold it with minimal content and a defined retention period. Record the shape of the
decision where you can, and keep the full detail only where the consequence justifies it.

## Where this shows in Lrnon

Certification is the clearest case. A result on a certificate says something about a
person. The project's design has the assessment reviewed by named people, who must be two
different individuals who have agreed on a set of sample answers before it opens. Results use
bands such as "not yet" and "nearly" and not a bare pass or fail, with a reviewer able to
upgrade a borderline one on request.

That is accountability built into the structure: the decision has an owner, there is a route
to reconsideration, and the language is chosen so that "not yet" is a position and not a
verdict.

The assistant is the reverse case, and is held back for a related reason. It produces
answers and not decisions about people, and it is still not open to learners until its
answers have been reviewed by hand, because someone must be able to stand behind what it
says.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

For an automation that affects people, answer in writing:

1. **Who is accountable,** by name, and can they change the output?
2. **How does an affected person reach a human,** and what exactly do they see?
3. **Can that human genuinely overrule it?**
4. **Could you explain a specific decision from last month using only the record?**

Where any answer is "not really", that is the next piece of work, and it is more urgent
than any improvement to the automation's accuracy.

## Recap

"The system decided" is an abdication: someone chose to build and rely on it, and that person
owes the affected an answer. Behind any automated decision put one named accountable person
with the authority to change it, a route to a human who can genuinely reconsider, and a
record written by your own code that lets a person explain what happened. The GDPR's
Article 22 reflects the same instinct for solely automated decisions with significant effects.
Lrnon's certification has an owner, bands rather than bare verdicts and a route to upgrade a
borderline result.
