A common order of events: someone dislikes a slow, irritating process and decides to
automate it. A tool is built. The process is now fast, irritating and set in code.

The fault was in the process, and it was never examined, because the automation went
straight for the symptom.

## Automation preserves what it finds

An automation does what the process does. If the process includes an unnecessary approval, a
duplicated entry or a step that exists because of a system retired five years ago, the
automation reproduces all of it, quickly and without complaint.

Worse, it makes the flaws harder to change. A manual process is adjusted by telling people.
An automated one needs someone to find the code, understand it and alter it, and for most
teams that someone is busy or has moved on. The fault is now infrastructure.

## Are the steps actually written down?

Test whether you know the process.

> Can a new colleague do this correctly using only the written steps?

If yes, you probably understand it. If not, one of two things is true: the steps are not
written, or the work depends on knowledge nobody has recorded. Both mean you cannot yet
tell an automation what to do, because you cannot tell a person.

Writing the steps down is not preparation for the project. It is often most of the benefit.
Teams who document a process properly frequently find they can cut a third of it before
touching any technology.

## Walk the real process, not the official one

The version on paper and the version people follow differ. Find the second.

**Shadow the work** (P9·L1). Sit with someone and watch. Notice every step they take that
the documentation omits: the message to a colleague, the spreadsheet they keep on the side,
the check they always make because it caught something once.

**Count the exceptions.** Pull a sample of recent cases and see how many were handled in the
standard way. If most were, the process is stable. If a large share needed a workaround, the
official process describes the easy cases, and an automation built from it will handle only
those.

Exceptions carry knowledge. Each is a trace of something the official process did not
anticipate, and often a clue to what the process is really for.

## Delete before you automate

For each step in the walked process, ask:

1. **Does anyone use its output?** Reports that nobody reads, fields nobody consults.
2. **Why does it exist?** If nobody knows, find out. It may be a protection (L1) or a leftover.
3. **Could two steps merge?**
4. **Could it happen earlier,** when the information is fresh, or later, when it is not needed?

A step you delete costs nothing to run, nothing to maintain and cannot go wrong. It is the
best automation there is.

Be careful with the second question. Deleting a step whose purpose you do not understand
is how protections disappear, and it is the reason to ask and to check with someone who has
been there longest.

## Stabilise it

A process that changes every month is a poor target. Each change requires the automation to
change with it.

If the process is still settling, such as a new product line or a reorganisation, wait. Run
it by hand until it stops moving, and write down what you learn. The automation you build
afterwards will be smaller and correct.

## What this looks like in miniature

Lrnon's own history has a version of this. The readiness gates in the admin console were
built before the process they were meant to represent was settled. They were a fixed list
that could not become true, representing a review process that did not yet exist as a habit.

The automation of the *display* ran ahead of the *process*. The mechanism for recording
reviews, and the gates computed from them, were built later and are only now meaningful, and
when this was written the process of actually reviewing answers had not started.

It would have been cheaper to settle what the review is, who does it, and how often, before
building the console around an imagined version.

## Try it now (30 minutes)

Choose a process you want to automate. Write the steps as they are really done, from
watching someone do it, and count what the official version leaves out.

Then mark every step with one of: keep, delete, merge, unsure. For each "unsure", find the
person who knows why it exists.

If more than a few steps are "unsure", the process is not ready.

## Recap

Automation preserves the flaws of what it finds and makes them harder to change. Test whether
a process is ready by asking whether a new colleague could follow it from the written steps.
Walk the real process, not the official one, and count the exceptions. Delete before you
automate, taking care over steps whose purpose nobody knows, since they may be protections.
Wait for a changing process to settle. Lrnon built a display for a review process that did
not yet exist as a habit.
