The useful thing to take from comparing two laws is that most of what they ask is the
same, so a workflow built with good habits meets much of both.

## What they share

> **Roles.** Someone decides why and how data is used and is answerable for it;
> someone else may process it for them.
>
> **A reason to use it.** You may not simply use personal data because you have it.
>
> **Telling people.** What you collect, why, and how to complain or withdraw.
>
> **Security.** Reasonable measures to prevent a breach.
>
> **Breach notification.** Telling the regulator and, in some cases, the people.
>
> **Erasure.** Deleting data when its purpose has ended, or when consent is withdrawn.
>
> **Children.** Extra care, and a parent's say for the youngest.
>
> **Reach beyond borders.** Both can apply to organisations outside their own
> territory.

If you build with all eight in mind, differences in detail become adjustments rather
than redesigns.

## Where they differ in ways that matter

**The reason to use data.** The GDPR names six lawful bases, among them consent,
contract, legal obligation and legitimate interests. The DPDP Act works through two
routes: consent, or a short list of listed *legitimate uses*. If you are used to
relying on a broad "legitimate interests" reason, there is no direct equivalent in the
Indian law.

**Special categories.** The GDPR gives extra protection to certain kinds of data,
such as health, beliefs and biometrics. The DPDP Act, as read for this lesson, does
not create a separate tier by category. That does not make health data safe to be
casual with; it means the extra care comes from the general duties and from sector
rules rather than from a special-category rule.

**Children.** Covered in L6. In short, the DPDP Act treats everyone under eighteen as
a child and also bars tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising aimed
at them. The GDPR's age for a child's own consent to online services is sixteen by
default, with member states free to lower it to no less than thirteen.

**Penalties.** Both are large, and they are structured differently.

| | Maximum |
|---|---|
| DPDP: failing to keep reasonable security safeguards | up to ₹250 crore |
| DPDP: failing to notify a breach, or children's obligations | up to ₹200 crore |
| DPDP: most other contraventions | up to ₹50 crore |
| GDPR: the most serious infringements (principles, rights, transfers) | up to €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher |
| GDPR: many operational duties (security, breach, impact assessments) | up to €10 million or 2%, whichever is higher |

Every one of those is a ceiling, and the DPDP figures read "may extend to". Real
penalties depend on the facts and on how the regulator weighs them. Treat the numbers
as a measure of how seriously the lawmakers regard the duty, and not as a prediction.

## Where each stands in time

**The GDPR** has applied since 2018 and is established, with years of regulator
decisions to read.

**The DPDP Act** is newer, and its position is more subtle than "in force" or "not".
It was passed in 2023 and its Rules were notified in November 2025. On the summaries
read for this lesson, commencement is staged:

> **Immediately** (November 2025): the parts setting up the Data Protection Board and
> the definitions.
>
> **After about twelve months** (around November 2026): the rules on registering
> consent managers, and one provision on consent withdrawal.
>
> **After about eighteen months** (around May 2027): the main duties on data
> fiduciaries, including notice, security safeguards, breach notification and
> children's data.

Those summaries disagree with each other by a day on the dates and on exactly which
sections fall in which phase. So the right reading is: the substantial obligations
described in this module are, as of 15 October 2026, **not yet in force in India**,
they are close, and the exact list should be taken from the Gazette notification.

That is no reason to wait. A workflow built now will still be running when they apply,
and retrofitting data minimisation into something already live is much harder than
building it in.

## Who is covered

Neither law stops at a border.

The **GDPR** applies to organisations established in the EU, and also to those
offering goods or services to people there or monitoring their behaviour there.

The **DPDP Act** applies to processing in India, and, as understood for this lesson,
to some processing outside India connected with offering goods or services to people in
India. The exact wording of that provision should be read.

A site anyone in the world can open, such as Lrnon, should therefore assume more than
one regime can be relevant, and build to the stricter habits. Which law actually
applies to a given project is a legal question, and the right answer needs advice.

## Recap

The two laws share a structure, namely roles, a reason to use data, notice, security,
breach notification, erasure, care for children and reach beyond borders, so good
habits meet much of both. They differ on lawful bases, special categories, the age of
a child and the shape of penalties, all of which are ceilings. The GDPR is established.
The DPDP Act's main duties were, on the summaries read, not yet in force on 15 October
2026 and arrive around May 2027, but a workflow built now will outlive that date.
Neither law stops at a border, and which one applies to you is a question for advice.
