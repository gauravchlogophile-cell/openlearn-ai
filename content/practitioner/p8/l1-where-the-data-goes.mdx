This module is about personal data in the workflows you build in P5 and P6, and
what two laws in particular, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act and the
EU's General Data Protection Regulation, ask of whoever builds them.

Read this first, because it shapes everything after.

> **This is general information and not legal advice.** Whether a law applies to
> you, and what it requires, depends on facts about your situation that a lesson
> cannot know. For a real obligation, ask someone qualified, and read the primary
> text.

## Start with the picture

Obligations attach to data you hold or send. So before any law, draw the path.

For a workflow, mark every place the data **enters**, **travels**, and **rests**:

> **Enters.** A form, an inbox, an upload, an API, a person typing a question.
>
> **Travels.** Each step of the chain, each connection, each call to the model.
>
> **Rests.** The database, the logs, the backups, the exports, the cache, the error
> reports, the analytics tool, and the copy someone saved to their own drive.

Then add the column that matters: **who holds it here, and do I control what they do
with it?**

## Personal data is wider than a name

It means information relating to an identifiable person. Identifiable includes being
named, and also being picked out: an email, a phone number, an account number, a
handle, a face, and often a free-text description that only one person fits.

Two consequences catch people.

**The question itself can be personal data.** Someone asking an assistant *"I have
chest pain and I'm 14, what should I do"* has just supplied health information and an
age. You asked for neither. Free-text boxes collect what people volunteer, and what
they volunteer is rarely what you planned for.

**"Anonymous" is a claim to test.** Removing a name does not make a record anonymous
if the remaining details still point at one person. A record with an age, a town and a
rare condition may be as identifying as one with a name.

## The roles

Both laws sort the parties by what they decide.

| | DPDP Act (India) | GDPR (EU) |
|---|---|---|
| Decides why and how data is used | **Data Fiduciary** | **Controller** |
| Handles it on their behalf | **Data Processor** | **Processor** |
| The person it is about | **Data Principal** | **Data Subject** |

That sorting decides who is answerable. Under the DPDP Act, section 8(1) makes the
Data Fiduciary responsible for compliance, including for processing done on its
behalf by a processor, *irrespective of any agreement to the contrary*. Hiring
someone to handle the data does not hand over the responsibility.

When you build a workflow that sends customer data to a model provider, you are
usually the fiduciary or controller, and the provider is a processor. L4 is about what
that makes you responsible for.

## A path with a gap in it

Lrnon's assistant is a useful picture because it is small enough to draw.

> A learner types a question.
> → It crosses a basic redaction step (L3).
> → It is sent, with four lesson passages, to an outside model provider.
> → An answer returns and is shown.
> → A row is written recording tokens, time, success and failure reason.

What rests where is worth stating. The row records no question and no learner
identity. The passages are public lessons. The question text exists in the request and
at the provider, and the provider's handling of it is governed by the provider's
terms, which L4 covers.

The point of drawing it is that the sensitive link in this chain is the one that
leaves Lrnon's control: the call to the provider. It does not appear in the database,
and it would be easy to leave off a diagram that began and ended at the project's own
systems.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

Pick one workflow. Draw it on paper with three columns: enters, travels, rests.

For every box, write who holds the data at that point and whether you control what
they do with it. Then circle every box that is **outside your systems**: a provider, an
analytics tool, a mailbox, someone's laptop.

The circled boxes are where L4 to L8 will apply. If you have none, check again. There
is nearly always at least one.

## Recap

This is general information, not legal advice, and it was checked on 15 October 2026
against reproductions and summaries rather than the official texts, so read those
before relying on a figure. Draw the path personal data takes: where it enters, where
it travels and where it rests, including the model provider, logs and backups. Personal
data is wider than a name, and the question a person types can itself be personal.
Both laws sort parties into the one who decides, the one who processes for them, and
the person concerned, and hiring a processor does not move the responsibility.
