Every duty in L2 is easier to meet, and cheaper to meet, if you hold less. Data you
never collected cannot be breached, does not have to be erased on request, and does
not need a contract with whoever would otherwise have received it.

That is **data minimisation**, and both laws state it as a duty. Here it is also just
good engineering.

## Ask what each step needs

For every step that receives personal data, ask three questions.

> **Does this step need it to do its job?**
> **Does it need all of it, or only part?**
> **Does it need it in this form, or would something less identifying do?**

A summarising step does not need the customer's name to summarise a complaint. A
classifier does not need an email address to decide the category. Strip what the step
does not need *before* it arrives, not after.

The most common finding when people do this honestly is that an identifier travelled
through the entire chain because it was in the first record, and nothing needed it
after step two.

## Replace identity with a reference

Often a step needs to refer to a person without knowing who they are.

Give the record an opaque key, a ticket number, an internal ID. The model sees *"ticket
4471 complains about a delayed refund"*, writes a reply, and your code, not the model,
joins it back to the customer. The model never held a name.

This is the same principle as P5·L4 and P6·L3 applied to privacy: the model supplies
content, and your code supplies the target. The identifying detail stays on the side of
the boundary you control.

## Prefer fields to free text

A free-text box is the most efficient way to collect more than you wanted.

People type what is on their mind. A "tell us more" field receives names, health
details, family circumstances and the occasional password. A set of structured fields,
a category, a date, a product, receives exactly the categories you asked for.

Where a free-text field is genuinely needed, tell people what not to put in it, next to
the field and in plain words, and accept that some will anyway.

## What redaction can and cannot do

When free text reaches a model, a redaction step is a sensible safety net. Be exact
about how small a net it is.

Lrnon's assistant redacts before a question is sent to the provider. It replaces
anything shaped like an email address, anything shaped like a phone number, and
grouped runs of twelve digits, with a plain marker. It runs on the line where the
question is handed over, because that is the last moment the text is still the
project's.

That catches things with a regular shape. It does **not** catch:

- **Names.** "My daughter Asha has been..." passes straight through.
- **Addresses** written in ordinary prose.
- **Health, family or financial details** described in words.
- **Anything where the pattern differs:** a number written with words, an email
  spelled out as "name at example dot com", an ID with a format nobody anticipated.
- **A description that identifies one person without naming them.**

So it reduces the obvious cases and leaves the rest. A pattern is a net, and nothing
about a net makes it a wall.

Two habits make it honest.

**Test it with real examples.** Feed it twenty things people actually type, and read
what survives. The result is nearly always shorter than the list of what you assumed
it did.

**Do not describe it as more than it is.** A notice that says personal data is removed
before it is sent is a claim, and the redactor does not support it. "We try to remove
obvious identifiers such as email addresses and phone numbers" is a claim it does.

## Say it where people type

The most effective control in a free-text assistant is often a sentence next to the box.

Lrnon's assistant never asks for personal details, which is part of its design, and its
footer now tells people not to type any. That is not a technical control and it should
not be the only one, but it changes what arrives. An assistant that says plainly *"don't
put personal details here"* receives fewer of them than one that does not.

## Purpose limitation

One more idea sits next to minimisation. Data collected for one purpose should not
quietly be used for another.

If you gather questions to answer them, do not later mine them to profile the people
who asked. In an automation this tends to happen sideways: a log written for debugging
becomes a dataset someone finds useful for something else. Decide the purpose when you
collect, write it down, and treat a new purpose as a new decision.

This is also why P3·L8 and P7·L4 recorded the *shape* of each call and not the
question. Not storing the content makes the repurposing impossible, which is stronger
than a policy against it.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

Take a workflow that handles personal data and, for each step, answer the three
questions at the top. Mark any step that receives more than it needs.

Then test any redaction you have with ten realistic inputs, including ones that contain
a name and an address. Write down what got through.

Finally find one free-text field you could replace with structured fields, or one place
to add a plain sentence saying what not to type.

## Recap

Hold and forward less: for each step ask whether it needs the data, how much, and in
what form, and strip the rest before it arrives. Use opaque references instead of
identities, and structured fields instead of free text where you can. A pattern-based
redactor catches emails and phone-like numbers and misses names, prose addresses and
described details, so test it with real examples and describe it as no more than a net.
A plain sentence next to the box changes what arrives, and not storing the content makes
repurposing it impossible.
