Sooner or later, someone writes: *"What do you hold about me?"* or *"Delete everything."*

At that moment the quality of everything in L1 to L5 shows. If you drew the map, kept
one identifier, set retention periods and minimised what you collected, the request is
an afternoon. If you did not, it is an investigation that may not be completable.

## The rights

Both laws give people a set of rights over their data. The wording and detail differ,
and the core is recognisable.

> **To know.** What you hold about them, why, and who has received it.
>
> **To correct.** Fix what is wrong or incomplete.
>
> **To erase.** Have it deleted, subject to exceptions.
>
> **To withdraw consent.** And have use stop, where consent was the basis.
>
> **To complain.** To you first, and then to the regulator.
>
> **Further, under the GDPR:** to receive their data in a portable form, to object to
> certain processing, to restrict it, and not to be subject to certain solely automated
> decisions with legal or similarly significant effect (Article 22).

Under the GDPR, a response is due without undue delay and in any case within one month,
extendable by up to two more months for complex or numerous requests, with the person
told of the extension and the reason. The DPDP Act's rights are in its Chapter III, and
the timetable and procedure are to be found in the Rules, which should be read directly.
For a precise period under either law, read the text and not this paragraph.

## What answering takes

Take the commonest request: *"What do you hold about me?"*

You need to be able to:

1. **Identify the person** confidently, and without handing their data to an impostor.
2. **Find every copy,** across the systems on your map.
3. **Assemble it** into something readable.
4. **Check what you may disclose,** since a record may also contain other people's data.
5. **Reply in time.**

Each step has a way to fail.

**Identity.** The request itself is a route to harm. Someone claiming to be a person to
obtain their data, or to have it deleted, is a real attack. Verify proportionately, and
do not ask for more identifying detail than you need to confirm who is asking.

**Completeness.** The ones you forget are the copies in logs, exports, backups and the
processor. If you answer from the main database alone, the answer is incomplete, and
being incomplete is worse than saying what you could not search.

**Other people.** A support thread quoting a colleague, a record shared between two
accounts. Redact what is not theirs.

## Erasure is the hard one

Access is mostly searching. Erasure is changing things, in many places, some of which are
difficult.

Work through it in the order of L5:

- **Delete from the main store** and every dependent record.
- **Remove from logs and exports** where they hold the personal data, or confirm they do
  not.
- **Handle backups** by letting them expire and re-applying the deletion on restore.
- **Ask the processor** to erase what it holds, where its terms allow.
- **Confirm,** by searching each place afterwards.
- **Tell the person** what was done, and what could not be, and why.

There are legitimate reasons not to erase something, such as a legal duty to keep it,
and the response should say so plainly rather than quietly omitting it.

## Make it routine, not heroic

A request should follow a known path.

**One page** describing the steps, who does them, and where each system's export and
delete commands are. Written before the first request.

**A log of requests,** with date received, action taken and date closed. It protects you
and shows the regulator you take it seriously.

**A named person** who owns it, and a backup.

**A rehearsal.** Pick a test account, ask the question, and see what is missing. This is
the quickest way to find the copy you forgot.

## Honest about the promise

Lrnon tells people a human answers feedback within two working days. That is a promise
about how fast a person replies, and it is not the legal period for a rights request,
which is different and depends on the law that applies.

The two should not be confused. A fast reply to a message is good practice. A rights
request has its own clock, its own record, and its own consequences if missed.

It is also worth saying what is easy for this project and what is not. A learner who
never signs in leaves almost nothing server-side, so there is little to find. A learner
who did sign in has an account, progress, and perhaps a volunteering record, held in
systems the map should list.

## Try it now (20 minutes)

Run a rehearsal. Use a test account, or yourself, and attempt to answer *"what do you
hold about me?"* across every place on your map.

Time it. Note every place you could not search, every export you could not produce, and
every system whose delete command you did not know.

Then write the one page. Include the missing places as "to do", because an honest list of
gaps is a better position than a confident one that is wrong.

## Recap

People can ask what you hold, to correct it, to have it erased, to withdraw consent and to
complain, and the GDPR adds portability, objection and a limit on solely automated
decisions. Answering needs a confident identity check, a search of every copy on your map,
care over other people's data, and a response in time under the law that applies. Erasure
is the hard case, touching stores, logs, backups and the processor, and ending in a
confirmation. Make it routine with a one-page procedure, a request log and a named owner,
and rehearse it on a test account.
