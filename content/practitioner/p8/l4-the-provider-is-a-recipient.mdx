When your workflow calls a model, whatever you send goes to someone else's computers.
In the language of both laws, that someone is a processor, and the act of sending is a
disclosure you are responsible for.

Most builders never read the terms. This lesson is about why the terms differ more than
people expect, and what that does to the promises you make.

## What both laws expect

**The GDPR** requires a binding written contract between a controller and a processor
(Article 28). It must set out the subject matter and duration of the processing, its
nature and purpose, the types of data and the categories of people, and the controller's
rights and duties. The controller may only use processors that give sufficient
guarantees, and a processor may not engage a further processor without the controller's
authorisation.

**The DPDP Act** lets a Data Fiduciary engage a Data Processor only under a valid
contract (section 8(2)), and keeps the fiduciary responsible for compliance whatever
that contract says (section 8(1)). Section 8(7) also requires the fiduciary to cause its
processor to erase data when the purpose has ended.

In practice, with a large model provider you do not negotiate a contract. You accept
its terms, and its data processing terms are the nearest thing to the agreement the laws
describe. The job is to read them.

## The plan changes the terms

This is the part people miss.

Many providers have separate terms for free and for paid use, and the differences
bear directly on privacy. For Google's Gemini API, the published terms, as excerpted for
this lesson, draw the line this way.

> **Unpaid use** (the free quota): submitted content and generated responses may be
> used to provide, improve and develop Google's products and machine learning
> technologies. Human reviewers may read, annotate and process input and output, after
> it has been disconnected from the account and key. Users are advised not to submit
> sensitive, confidential or personal information.
>
> **Paid use:** prompts and responses are described as not used to improve Google's
> products, and are logged for a limited period for detecting abuse and meeting legal
> disclosures.

This is a paraphrase, not a quotation. It was checked on 15 October 2026 against
Google's own terms page, which showed a last-updated date of 28 April 2026, and providers
revise these documents, so read the current page for your plan. The lesson is the shape. **The same API call, with the same code, can be
under quite different data terms depending on whether billing is switched on.**

Other providers draw the lines elsewhere, and some distinguish consumer products from
business or API access. Read the terms for the plan you are actually on, and read them
again when the plan or the terms change.

## A case from this project

Lrnon's assistant footer, on every answer, used to end with *"Nothing you type is used
to train anything."*

That sentence was written as reassurance and it was never checked. The question goes to
an outside provider, and the quota errors seen in testing, about ten calls a minute and
a message to check the plan and billing, are consistent with the free tier. Whether the
project's key is on the free tier had not been confirmed when this was written.

If it is, the provider's own terms contradict the footer. The sentence promised learners
something that Lrnon had no authority to promise, about the questions of people who
include children.

The footer now says only what holds on any plan: that the question is sent to an outside
AI service, and that people should not type personal details. The comment beside the
string records why, and says to restore a training claim only after the plan and its
current terms have been checked and dated.

The generalisable rule:

> **An assurance to users must be true on the plan you actually use, today.** If it
> depends on a setting you have not checked, you do not yet have the assurance.

## What to read in the terms

For any provider, find answers to these, and write down where you found them and when.

1. **Is submitted content used to train or improve the provider's products?** On which
   plans?
2. **Can people at the provider read it?** Under what conditions?
3. **How long is it kept,** and why?
4. **Where is it processed?** Regions matter for cross-border transfer rules, and for
   whether a provider serves your users at all (P7·L6, and P10).
5. **Who are the sub-processors?**
6. **Can you delete it,** and how do you ask?
7. **What happens to your data if you stop using the service?**

If the answers are not findable, that is itself an answer.

## What to do about it

**Match the plan to the data.** If the work involves personal data from other people, a
plan whose terms tell you not to submit personal data is the wrong plan. Moving to a paid
tier, or to a provider with business terms, is often cheap against the risk.

**Or change the data.** L3's measures reduce what is sent, so that the plan matters less.
Neither replaces the other.

**Say what is true.** Whatever you tell users should survive being read next to the
provider's terms.

**Keep a dated note** of what the terms said and what plan you were on. When an
assurance is challenged, "we checked on 15 October and this is what it said" is a very
different position from "we assumed".

## Try it now (15 minutes)

For a provider you use, find the data terms for your plan and answer the seven
questions above, with the date.

Then read every statement you have made to users about their data, in your footer, your
privacy page, your help text, and test each one against the answers. Anything that
depends on something you did not check is a promise to remove or verify.

## Recap

A model provider is a recipient, and both laws expect an agreement governing what it may
do while keeping you answerable. With a large provider the agreement is its terms, which
often differ by plan: on a free tier, content may be used to improve products and read by
reviewers, while paid terms commonly say otherwise. Lrnon's own footer promised no
training without that having been checked. Read the terms for the plan you use, answer
seven specific questions with the date, match the plan to the data, and make every
statement to users true on that plan.
