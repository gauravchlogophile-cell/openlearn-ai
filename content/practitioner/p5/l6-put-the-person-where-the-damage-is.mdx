P3·L6 said that irreversible actions should be proposed by a model and
authorised by a person. In an automation that sentence becomes a design
question, because the whole purpose of the chain was to remove the person.

So where do you put them back?

## Not at the start

The tempting place is the beginning. *"A human reviews the incoming request, and
then the automation takes over."*

That reviews the wrong thing. At the start, nobody can see what the chain will
do. They are approving an input, and the model has not yet produced the draft,
chosen the label, or decided the amount. By the time something consequential
happens, the person's judgement is several steps behind.

Put the human **immediately before the action that cannot be undone**, with the
real consequence in front of them.

## What the approval shows

A weak approval says *"Send reply?"* and offers two buttons.

A real one shows the effect:

> **To:** m.rao@customer.example
> **Subject:** Re: Refund for order 4471
> **Body:** (the full text)
> **Also:** marks ticket 4471 as resolved, issues a refund of £38.00

Three properties make it work.

**It names the specifics.** Recipient, amount, which record. The wrong thing has
to be legible in it, or approving is just agreeing with the arrow.

**Refusal is cheap and normal.** If declining takes four clicks and a reason,
people approve to avoid the trouble.

**It does not batch.** Ten actions behind one button is a single decision about
ten things, and the ninth is where the surprise sits.

## The reflex problem

Every approval step has a half-life. The first week people read each one. By the
fourth they approve at a glance. By the tenth the approval is a reflex, and the
step remains in the diagram, still looking like a safeguard, doing nothing.

This is not laziness. It is the predictable result of asking a person to judge
the same thing forty times a day, where nearly all are fine.

The fix is to ask less, not to exhort harder. **Auto-handle the clear cases and
send only the unusual to a person.**

## Escalate by rule, not by confidence

The obvious way to pick which cases go to a human is to ask the model how sure
it is. Avoid it. A model's stated confidence is a poor guide to whether it is
right, and an injected instruction can lower or raise it at will.

Use rules, which are checkable and cannot be talked round:

- The amount is over a ceiling.
- The recipient has never been written to before.
- The message came from outside the company domain.
- The category came back as *other*.
- It is outside working hours.
- The text contains a link, or an attachment.

Each is a condition that a plain step can test. Routine cases flow through, the
ones with a reason to look get a person, and the person sees few enough that
they read them.

## Who approves, and what happens if nobody does

**The approver has to be able to judge.** Not whoever is nearest. A person
signing off a refund needs the order history in front of them, or the approval
is a rubber stamp with a name on it.

**Silence must mean no.** If a request waits a day and then proceeds, it was
never an approval. It was a delayed action with a pretence of oversight. Set the
timeout to *do nothing* and tell somebody it is waiting.

**Name a backup.** An approval that depends on one person who is on leave is
indistinguishable from a broken chain.

## The same rule at the project level, and where it is only half built

Lrnon's rollout console follows the asymmetry. Turning Sky **off** is open to any
administrator and needs nobody's approval, because a kill switch that requires
sign-off is not one. Widening it to **everyone** is for the owner alone.

The principle is to make the dangerous direction hard and the safe direction
instant. Part of this is real, and part is not, and the difference is the useful
bit.

The console says opening Sky to everyone "needs a second owner to approve", and
the button is disabled until four readiness gates are met. When this lesson was
written, the database function behind that button was read, and it checks only
that the caller is the owner. It does not look for a second approver and it does
not look at the gates. Both exist in the page and nowhere else.

That is a lock in the interface, which is a hint rather than a control. The
practical risk is small, since the one person able to call it is the person who
would also approve it. The lesson is the general one from P3·L6: a safeguard is
only as real as the layer that enforces it, and it is worth reading the layer
behind the button before describing what the button does.

## Try it now (10 minutes)

Pick the most consequential action in a chain you have drawn. Write what the
approval screen would show, field by field.

Then count how often it would fire in a normal week. If the answer is more than
a handful, write the rule that would let the routine cases through, and keep
the person for the rest.

Finally, state what happens at the timeout. If it is anything other than nothing,
change it.

## Recap

Place the person immediately before the action that cannot be undone, not at the
start of the chain. Show the real effect, make refusal cheap, and never batch.
Approvals decay into reflexes if they fire constantly, so auto-handle the clear
cases and send only the unusual to a human, chosen by rules rather than by the
model's confidence. Make sure the approver can judge, name a backup, and let
silence mean no. Make the dangerous direction hard and the safe direction
instant.
