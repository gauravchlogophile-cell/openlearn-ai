Every lesson so far has been about building an automation. This one is about the
longest part of its life, which is everything after.

An automation is built in an afternoon and runs for years. The attention it
received at the start is the most it will ever get, and the gap between the two
is where problems live.

## How they get forgotten

The mechanism is unremarkable.

You build one. It works. You stop thinking about it, which is its entire purpose.
Eighteen months later you have moved teams. The inbox it watches has been
reorganised. The folder it writes to now holds something else. The connection
still works, holding the same broad permission it was given, as a person who
no longer works on this.

Nothing has failed. It is just running, on assumptions that stopped being true.

## Keep a list

Start with the simplest thing, because everything else depends on it.

A list of every automation you have: **a name, an owner, what it does, what it
touches.** One row each.

It will feel pointless while you have three. It becomes the only way to answer
two important questions once you have thirty:

- **What would be affected if this account were revoked?**
- **What do we have that touches customer data?**

You cannot answer either from memory. Platforms will show you the workflows
inside them, and none will show you the ones in another tool, a colleague's
account, or a scheduled script on someone's laptop. Those are usually the
forgotten ones.

## One page for each

For anything that matters, write a single page. It should answer five questions:

1. **What does it do?** In a sentence a stranger would follow.
2. **What does it touch?** Accounts, files, services, and what it can change.
3. **Who owns it?** A name, with a backup.
4. **How do I stop it?** Exactly, in order.
5. **When was it last reviewed?** A date.

Keep it short enough that somebody will actually write it. A page that exists is
worth ten that were planned.

The fourth question is the valuable one. It is written in calm and read in a
hurry, and the person reading may not be the person who built the chain.

## A review date

Put a date on the calendar, six months out, with one purpose: *is this still
needed, still correct, and still at the right scope?*

The questions are quick.

- **Needed?** Does anybody still use the output? If nobody would notice it
  stopping, that is information.
- **Correct?** Has the process around it changed? A chain built for last year's
  refund policy is wrong now, and quietly.
- **Scoped?** Does it still need every permission it holds? Permissions are easy
  to grant and rarely revisited.

Lrnon applies a version of this to its content. A tool description older than 120
days fails the build, so staleness stops a deploy instead of waiting to be
noticed. Most automations have nothing like that, which is why the date has to be
written down by a person and not left to the platform.

## Retire cleanly

When an automation has finished its job, turn it off properly. There are four
steps, and people usually do the first.

1. **Disable the trigger.**
2. **Revoke its connections** at the service itself, not only in the platform.
3. **Remove it from the list**, with a note of when and why.
4. **Delete or archive the credentials** it held.

Skipping the second and fourth leaves a live key with no owner and no purpose,
which is the least useful thing to own. A disabled workflow with its connection
still active is a standing grant waiting for anyone who finds the credential.

## Who runs the platform itself?

One more owner is easy to miss: whoever operates the platform.

If you use a hosted service, it is the provider, and you are trusting them to
patch and secure it. If you self-host, as the free option for n8n allows, it is
you: updates, backups, access control, and uptime. Self-hosting is not free in
effort. It trades a bill for an obligation, and the obligation lands on the same
person who is also the owner of every chain.

Neither is wrong. They are different ways of owning the same problem, and the
inventory should say which one you chose.

## The honest version for Lrnon

Lrnon has one maintainer. The deployment pipeline, the database, the secrets, the
assistant and the rollout console all rest with a single person, and the brand
guides say so in the same plain terms.

That is a fair account of the **bus factor** here, and it is the most important
fact about the project's automation. Nothing about it is hidden, but the risk is
real: if that person is unavailable, the pipeline runs unreviewed, and whatever
is not written down is lost with them.

That is why this project keeps its decisions in commit messages, its test
rationale in the tests, and its guides in the repository. Not as virtue. As the
inventory and the one-pagers, done in the only form that survives one person.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

Make the list. Write down every automation you can think of, including the ones
on someone else's account and any that run on a schedule you set up once.

For each, fill in owner and purpose. Then look for two kinds of row.

- **No owner.** Assign one, today, or retire it.
- **No answer to "who would notice if it stopped?"** That is a candidate for the
  retirement steps above.

Write the one-pager for the most consequential, and put a review date in the
calendar.

## Recap

Automations run for years and receive their most attention on the first day.
Keep a list with an owner, a purpose and what each touches, because it is the
only way to answer what a revoked account would affect. Write a one-page
description including how to stop it, set a six-monthly review for need,
correctness and scope, and retire cleanly: disable the trigger, revoke the
connection at the service, update the list and delete the credentials. Record
who operates the platform itself, because every choice transfers the obligation
somewhere.
