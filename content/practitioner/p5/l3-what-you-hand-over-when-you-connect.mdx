The moment an automation does anything useful, it has to be allowed to. It needs
to read a mailbox, write to a sheet, post to a channel, call an API. The step
where you permit that is a few clicks and a consent screen, and it is the most
consequential thing in the whole build.

Connecting an account is **granting authority**. P3·L4 called the assistant a
confused deputy: it holds your credentials and acts on text it did not write.
This lesson is about deciding how much it holds.

## Read the scope line

Most connections use a permission request listing exactly what is being granted.
The wording varies; the pattern does not.

> *"See and download your files"*
> *"Read, compose, send and permanently delete all your email"*
> *"Post messages to channels you can access"*

Notice how different the first and second are, and how little the consent screen
conveys it. A scope is a grant to the automation of everything that line allows,
for as long as the connection exists, performed as you.

Ask one question of every line: **does this chain need that, or is it simply the
option the platform offered?**

Platforms tend to request broad scopes because broad scopes work for everyone.
If your automation only needs to *read* a mailbox and the request says *read and
send*, you have granted a send capability you do not need. Many services offer a
narrower option if you look. Where they do not, that is itself worth knowing
before you build on it.

## Whose account is it?

The easy path is to connect your own. It works immediately and costs nothing.
It also causes three problems, none of which show up on day one.

**It carries your full reach.** The automation can touch everything you can,
including things unrelated to its job. A chain that only needed one folder has
been given your whole drive.

**Everything is attributed to you.** Messages it posts and records it edits show
your name. When something goes wrong, you are the person who did it.

**It leaves when you do.** The day your account is suspended, the chain stops, or
quietly runs with stale permissions, and nobody knows it was yours to move.

The alternative is a **dedicated account**: a shared mailbox, a service user, an
account created for this purpose with only the access the chain needs. It takes
twenty minutes to set up and belongs to the team, not to a person.

## Where the keys live

An automation platform stores your credentials so it can use them later. That is
what it is for, and it has a consequence worth saying plainly.

**The platform now holds the keys to everything you connected.** If it is
compromised, or someone with access to it is careless, the blast radius is every
connected service at once.

This is not an argument against them. It is an argument for knowing what you
connected, and for choosing the platform with that in mind. Self-hosting changes
*who the operator is*; it does not remove the operator. Running n8n on your own
server means the credential store is yours to protect, update and back up, which
is a real saving in some respects and a real obligation in others. A hosted
service means somebody else carries that, and you are trusting them to.

Neither is safer by default. The honest comparison is which operator you trust
to do that job well.

## Keep them out of the wrong rooms

Lrnon's own build taught this directly. The assistant's API key was, for a time,
entered as a *build* variable rather than a *runtime* secret. The difference
matters: a build variable is present in the environment while dependencies are
installed and the site is built, where any package that runs a script during
installation can read it. A runtime secret is never there.

Nothing suggests the key leaked. It was in a room it had no reason to be in, and
moving it cost two minutes. The general rule is the one from P3·L6: a credential should be
present only where something genuinely needs it, and absent everywhere else.

## One credential per job

If five automations share one connection, revoking it for one revokes it for all,
and a flaw in any one exposes the others. Give each its own, so each can be cut
off alone and each reveals only what it was given.

## Know how to cut it off

Before you rely on any automation, answer this: **if it began misbehaving right
now, what is the single action that stops it from touching anything?**

Revoking the connection at the service itself is the strongest answer, because
it works even if the platform is unavailable. Disabling the workflow in the
platform is faster but depends on the platform. Know both, and know where each
lives.

## Try it now (10 minutes)

Open your automation platform and list every connected account. For each, write:

1. **The scope it holds.** Look it up; do not assume.
2. **Whose account it is.** A person, or a dedicated one?
3. **What would be lost if it were revoked tonight.** A shorter list than expected
   usually means an unused connection you can remove now.

Remove anything unused immediately. An idle connection with broad access costs
nothing to keep and is entirely downside.

## Recap

Connecting an account grants authority, so read each scope line and take the
narrowest that works. Prefer a dedicated account to your own, which carries your
whole reach and leaves when you do. The platform holds the keys to everything
connected, and self-hosting only changes who operates that store. Give each
automation its own credential, keep keys out of places that do not need them, and
know the single action that cuts an automation off.
