The moment a colleague starts relying on your assistant, it stops being a thing
you made and becomes **infrastructure**. Not because it is important, but because
people depend on its behaviour without reading it.

That changes what an edit means. A document someone edits badly is visibly
different. A configuration someone edits badly produces answers that are subtly
different for everyone, immediately, with no review step and nobody told.

## Three failures, all ordinary

None of these needs anybody to behave unreasonably.

**The helpful tightening.** Someone adds *"always respond in under 100 words"*
because an answer was long. Reasonable. It also truncates the one case where
detail mattered, and nobody connects the two.

**The scope creep.** A colleague asks whether it can also do X. Someone adds X
to the instructions. Six months later it does nine things adequately, having
done one thing well — the L2 failure, arrived at by increments nobody would
refuse individually.

**The silent fix that moved the problem.** An instruction is rewritten to solve
one complaint. It solves it. It also changes behaviour on a case the author
never tried, and the person who relied on that case now has a tool that is
quietly worse and no vocabulary for saying so.

What these share is that nothing looks broken. There is no error, no failure, no
log line. Just a tool that fits someone's work slightly less well than it did
last month.

## Name an owner

One person. Not a committee, and not "the team".

The owner's job is narrow: decide what the assistant is for, and keep it being
that. In practice that means saying no to additions — which is why it has to be
a person rather than a group, because a group approves things.

If nobody owns it, the honest statement is that it is nobody's job to notice it
has drifted, and nobody will.

## Decide who may edit, explicitly

Most platforms offer roughly: only me, some people, anyone in the organisation.
Pick deliberately, and understand what you are choosing.

**Narrow editing is usually right** for anything colleagues depend on. The cost
is that improvements wait for the owner. The benefit is that behaviour does not
change without someone deciding it should.

**Wide editing suits a shared scratchpad** — something a small group iterates on
together, where everyone using it is also maintaining it. That is a real and
good use, and it is a different kind of object from the one people depend on.

The failure is not choosing: leaving the default, discovering months later that
four people have edited it, and having no way to find out what any of them
changed.

## Keep the text somewhere you control

This is the practical heart of the lesson, and it is almost always skipped.

**Most platforms keep no usable history of your instructions.** There is no diff,
no previous version, no "who changed this". The box contains whatever it
contains now.

So keep your own copy. In order of preference:

- **In version control**, as a plain text file. You get history, diffs,
  authorship and dates for free, and the file is the source of truth that gets
  pasted in rather than the box being the original.
- **In a dated document**, appending a copy each time it changes.
- **At minimum**, one line per change in a log: date, what changed, why.

That last one takes ten seconds and is the difference between "it used to work"
being a diagnosis and being a complaint.

## The question you are preparing to answer

Someone will say: *"this was better a few weeks ago."* They will be vague,
because they are describing a feeling about output quality and have no example
to hand.

With a record, that is tractable. You look at what changed, find the
tightening, and either explain the trade or revert it.

Without one, you have an unfalsifiable claim about a tool whose behaviour you
cannot reconstruct — and you will probably conclude they are misremembering,
which is the worst available outcome because it is sometimes true and you have
no way to tell.

## Lrnon's version, including the bit that failed

Sky's instructions live in `src/lib/sky-providers.ts`, in version control, with
every change in the history and a commit message saying why. The September
change to rule 3 is a single diff, dated, with the measured reason recorded
beside it.

That is the good version. The bad version happened too, and it is worth naming
because it is the same class of problem one level up. The readiness gates in the
admin console were a hard-coded list with three of four permanently false — so
no amount of actual review would have moved them. A gate that cannot change is
not a control, and nobody noticed for weeks, because nothing about a false value
looks wrong.

Version control answers *what changed*. It does not answer *whether the thing
was ever capable of changing*, and that question needs asking separately.

## Try it now (8 minutes)

For one shared assistant, write down three things:

1. **Who owns it** — a name, not a team.
2. **Who may edit it** — and go and check the actual setting rather than
   assuming.
3. **Where the instruction text is kept** outside the platform.

If the third is blank, fix it now by copying the text into a file with today's
date. That copy is worth more than it looks: it is the only thing that makes the
next change reversible.

## Recap

Once colleagues depend on it, a configuration is infrastructure: edits change
behaviour for everyone at once, with no review and no announcement. The usual
damage comes from reasonable changes — a helpful tightening, an agreed addition,
a fix that moved the problem. Name one owner, choose the editing permission
deliberately rather than leaving the default, and keep the instruction text
somewhere with history. That copy is what makes "it used to work" a diagnosis
instead of a complaint.
