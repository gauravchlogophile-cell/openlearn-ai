P1 taught you to write a good prompt. This is a different skill, and the
difference is not length.

When you prompt, you are in the loop. You read the answer, notice it missed the
point, and say so. The second turn fixes the first — P1·L5 argued that the
second prompt matters most, and it does.

A configuration gets no second turn. Someone asks it one question, reads one
answer, and forms a view about whether this thing is any good. You are not
there. You will usually never know what they asked.

## Write for the worst reasonable user

Not a hostile one — P3 covered that. Someone **busy, unfamiliar, and reasonable**:
they will ask in half a sentence, they will not read your description, they will
assume it can do more than it can, and they will not come back to tell you it
went wrong. They will simply stop using it.

Three things follow.

### Say what the job is, in the first line

Not a personality. A job.

> *"You are a helpful assistant for the marketing team."*

This rules nothing out. Compare:

> *"You rewrite draft copy to match the house style guide below. You do not
> invent claims, prices or statistics, and you do not write anything that has
> not been asked for."*

The second constrains. A personality decorates an answer; a job constrains it —
and constraint is the whole value of handing over a configuration rather than
a paragraph of encouragement.

### Say what to do when the request is unclear

This is the single most skipped instruction, and the one that most changes how a
shared assistant behaves in practice.

A personal prompt does not need it: when you are vague, you see the misreading
and correct it. A colleague who is vague gets a confident answer to a question
they did not ask, and has no idea.

So choose, deliberately:

> *"If the request is ambiguous, ask one clarifying question before answering.
> If it is outside rewriting copy — anything about budgets, contracts or
> people — say that is outside what you do, and suggest who to ask."*

Both halves matter. Without the first, it guesses. Without the second, it
helpfully ventures into territory where being wrong costs something.

### Say what good looks like, with an example

P1·L1 argued for showing rather than telling, and it is more important here,
because the reader of your instructions is a model and the users of your
assistant are not available to interpret.

One worked example — a realistic input and the output you want — does more than
three paragraphs of description. Two examples are better, especially when the
second is an edge case: a request that should be refused, or a short input that
should produce a short answer rather than a padded one.

## Keep the scope narrower than you want to

The temptation when building is to make it handle everything. Resist it, for a
reason that is about people rather than models.

An assistant that does one thing well is used. One that does six things
adequately is tried once and abandoned, because a user who gets a mediocre
answer on their first attempt does not file a bug — they conclude the tool is
mediocre, and that conclusion is very hard to reverse.

Narrow also makes the next two lessons possible: a narrow assistant can be
tested (L6), and its knowledge can be kept current (L3). A broad one cannot
really be either.

## Name the audience inside the instructions

Worth stating explicitly, because models pitch to whoever they are told about:

> *"The people asking are support agents with product knowledge but no legal
> training. Do not explain what a refund is. Do not offer legal interpretation."*

That single sentence removes both over-explanation and over-reach, which are the
two most common complaints about a shared assistant.

## Try it now (12 minutes)

Take a prompt you use regularly and convert it into a configuration. Add the
three things a personal prompt never needs:

1. The job, as a constraint, in the first line.
2. What to do when a request is unclear, and what is out of scope.
3. One worked example, plus one that should be refused.

Then do the test that matters: **give it to a colleague with no explanation at
all.** Not a description, not a demo — just the assistant. What they ask first is
almost never what you designed for, and that gap is the thing you are actually
writing against.

## Recap

A prompt is steered and a configuration is handed over, so write for someone
busy, unfamiliar and reasonable who will ask once and not come back. Open with
the job as a constraint rather than a personality, say what to do when a request
is unclear and what lies outside, and show one good example and one refusal.
Keep the scope narrower than you want, because narrow is what makes an assistant
usable, testable and possible to keep true.
