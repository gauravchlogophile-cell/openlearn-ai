P2·L4 made you build twenty questions with known answers to find out whether
retrieval worked. P3·L7 made you build an attack corpus. This is the same move
for a configuration, and it is smaller: a dozen questions is enough.

The argument is identical in all three places. A thing you have not measured is
a thing you are guessing about, and the guess is always flattering.

## Why your own testing is the weakest kind

You are the worst available tester of your own assistant, for three reasons that
have nothing to do with care.

**You ask in its vocabulary.** You wrote the instructions, so you use the words
they use. A colleague says *"sort this out for me"* and attaches something, and
that is a genuinely harder input than anything you typed.

**You know the boundaries.** You do not ask the out-of-scope question, because
you know it is out of scope. The colleague does not know, so it is the second
thing they try.

**You read a near-miss as a hit.** You know what the answer should have been, so
an answer gesturing at it feels right. Someone without that knowledge reads only
what is on the screen.

None of this is fixable by being more careful. It is fixable by writing the
cases down in advance and by letting someone else use the thing.

## The dozen

Four of each, roughly:

### Four it should handle well

The real work. Take actual requests — from an inbox, a ticket queue, a chat —
rather than inventing clean ones. Invented requests are always better phrased
than real ones.

Write down what a good answer contains. Not the exact words; the facts it must
include and the things it must not.

### Four that are awkward

This is where the information is.

- **Badly phrased.** A half-sentence, no context, a typo in the key term.
- **Adjacent.** Plausibly related, actually outside the scope. For a copy
  rewriter: *"is this claim legally OK?"*
- **Underspecified.** Missing the one thing needed to answer. Does it ask, as
  L2's instruction told it to, or does it guess?
- **Too long.** A wall of pasted text with the real question in the middle.

### Four it should refuse

Deliberate, written down, with the refusal you want.

What counts as refusable depends on the assistant, and deciding is most of the
value: a copy assistant should refuse to invent a statistic; a support assistant
should refuse to promise a refund; any assistant pointed at the public should
refuse medical, legal and financial advice and say who to ask.

A refusal case passes when it refuses **and says what to do instead**. A bare
"I can't help with that" is a dead end, and a dead end gets worked around rather
than respected.

## Hand it to one person and watch

The highest-value twenty minutes available: give it to a colleague, with no
explanation, and watch without helping.

Resist explaining. The urge is strong and it destroys the evidence — once you
have said "try asking it to rewrite something", you have lost the thing you were
measuring, which is what somebody does with no instructions.

Two things usually happen. They ask something you did not design for, and they
abandon a question halfway and rephrase it. Both are findings. The second is the
more useful one, because it tells you the first answer was discouraging in a way
no test case would have shown.

## Re-run it, and know what forces a re-run

The set is worth keeping because the things that break an assistant arrive
quietly:

- **An instruction edit** — including one that looks cosmetic.
- **A knowledge update**, which changes what retrieval surfaces.
- **The model changing underneath you.** The platform updates, or a version
  alias moves, and nothing on your side changed at all.

That last one is why a periodic run matters and not only a run after edits. It
is also measured rather than theoretical: Sky's own injection corpus has a case
that passed four times and failed the fifth with nothing changed in between —
one in five, from the same inputs. Four passes were not evidence that it worked.
They were four samples.

If one case matters more than the rest, run it several times before believing it.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

Write twelve lines. One per case: the question, and what a good answer contains.

Run them. Mark each pass, fail, or arguable — and keep the arguable ones rather
than resolving them, because they are where your instructions are ambiguous
rather than wrong.

Then the question worth answering honestly: **how many of these did you try
before reading this?** For most assistants in use, the answer is the four easy
ones, which is also the four that told you least.

## Recap

Write a dozen cases with the good answer recorded: four it should handle, four
awkward, four it should refuse with somewhere to go instead. Your own testing is
the weakest kind, because you ask in its vocabulary and read near-misses
generously, so hand it to one colleague and watch without explaining. Re-run
after any edit and periodically regardless, since the model can change with
nothing changed on your side — and run the case you most rely on several times,
because one pass is one sample.
