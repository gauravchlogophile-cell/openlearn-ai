Everything so far has been about producing an answer. This lesson is about the
answer you do not produce, which is the part that decides whether anyone should
trust the rest.

## The failure this exists for

L1 named it and L3 measured it. Retrieval **narrows**; it does not
**adjudicate**. A passage can be the most relevant thing you have and still not
contain the answer.

Lrnon's own case is the clean example: *"What is the capital of France?"*
retrieves E1·L6, because that lesson contains the phrase as a
next-word-prediction example. Retrieval did its job correctly. The passage does
not answer the question. Something else has to notice.

## An instruction is a request

The obvious move is to tell the model:

> *"If the passages do not contain the answer, say so and stop."*

Write it. It genuinely helps. But understand what it is: a request, complied
with most of the time, and most of the time is not a property you can build on.
The pull toward being helpful is strong, and a model that has read four
passages about a topic will often produce something.

> **A prompt is a request. A return statement is not.**

If refusing matters — and in a grounded system it is the whole point — then
something after the answer has to be able to throw it away.

## The check that costs almost nothing

You already asked for citations in L5. Use them:

> **Discard any answer that does not cite a passage you actually supplied.**

Two properties make this good. It is cheap — a regular expression and a range
check. And it is *aligned with the failure*: an answer built from general
knowledge rather than the passages has nothing legitimate to cite, so it
either cites nothing or fabricates a reference, and both are caught.

This is what Lrnon's assistant does. An answer citing nothing, or citing `[7]`
when four passages were supplied, never reaches the learner — they get the
out-of-scope response instead. The promise "never guesses" stops depending on
the model's cooperation.

## Refuse before spending, where you can

Not every refusal needs a model. If retrieval returns nothing above threshold,
you already know the answer is not there. Refuse then — before the call, which
is the only moment refusing is free.

The order is worth stating generally: **cheapest and most decisive checks
first.** Out of scope, refuse. Nothing retrieved, refuse. Only then spend.

## What a refusal owes the person

A refusal that leaves someone worse off than silence is a bad refusal. "I don't
know" hands the problem straight back with nothing added.

A good one carries three things:

> **What was searched.** "I only read Lrnon's own pages" tells them the shape
> of the gap — this is not a knowledge failure, it is a scope boundary.
>
> **Why it fell short.** Not covered here, versus covered but too old, versus
> a question that needs a person, are three different situations.
>
> **Where to go next.** A search box, a contact route, a related page. Somebody
> stuck needs a next step more than an apology.

Notice what is absent: apology, hedging, and any suggestion the person asked
badly.

## Do not fall back to general knowledge

The most tempting mistake in this whole module: when the passages fall short,
let the model answer from what it knows anyway.

This un-grounds the answer while keeping every visual signal of a grounded one
— the same interface, the same confident tone, often the same source links
sitting underneath text those sources do not support. You have built something
that looks checkable and is not, which is worse than something that never
claimed to be.

If you want an ungrounded mode, label it loudly as a different thing.

## Try it now (7 minutes)

Ask your grounded system three questions your documents definitely do not
answer — adjacent topics work best, because they retrieve *something*.

For each, check: did it refuse, or did it construct an answer from marginally
related passages? If it answered, you have found the gap this lesson exists for,
and the citation check is the cheapest thing that closes it.

## Recap

Retrieval narrows but never adjudicates, so something must decide when the
passages fall short. Instruct the refusal, then enforce it: discard any answer
that does not cite a passage you supplied. Refuse before spending where you
can. And make the refusal useful — what was searched, why it fell short, where
to go next — while never quietly falling back on general knowledge.
