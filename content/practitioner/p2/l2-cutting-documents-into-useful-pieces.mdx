You cannot send the whole handbook with every question. Even where the context
window allows it, you pay for every token and you make the relevant sentence
harder to locate rather than easier.

So documents get cut into passages. How you cut them decides almost everything
about how well the finished thing works, and it is the step people spend the
least time on.

## The two failures

**Too small.** A passage of one sentence often cannot be understood alone.

> *"This does not apply to orders placed before that date."*

Which date? What does not apply? Retrieved on its own, this is noise — and
worse, it is *confident-looking* noise that a model will happily build an
answer on.

**Too large.** A passage of thirty pages technically contains the answer, and
also contains four other things that look similar. Precision collapses, cost
rises, and you have moved the needle-in-a-haystack problem rather than solved
it.

The useful size is usually a few hundred words: one idea, stated completely.

## Cut on structure, not on length

The common first attempt is to split every 1,000 characters. It is easy, and
it is wrong for a reason worth understanding: 1,000 characters lands wherever
it lands. Mid-sentence, mid-table, between a heading and the paragraph it
introduces.

Documents already tell you where topics change. Headings, sections, clause
numbers, list items — these are the author marking the boundaries for you.

> **Cut at the structure the document already has.** Then, if a section is
> still too long, split it at paragraph boundaries and repeat its heading at
> the top of each piece.

That last part matters more than it sounds. A passage lifted from under
"Section 4 — Refunds" loses the word "refunds" the moment it is separated. Carry
the heading down into the piece and the passage keeps its subject.

## Overlap, and when it is not a fix

A common technique is to overlap consecutive passages by a sentence or two, so
an idea straddling a boundary appears whole in at least one of them.

It helps. It is also frequently reached for to paper over cutting at the wrong
places to begin with — if your boundaries follow the document's structure, an
idea rarely straddles one. Use overlap for genuine spillover, not as a
substitute for cutting well.

## The test

There is one test, and it takes ten seconds per passage:

> **Read the passage with nothing around it. Can you tell what it is about?**

If you cannot, retrieval cannot either, because retrieval sees exactly what you
just saw. A passage that only makes sense in context will be matched on the
wrong questions and will mislead the model when it is supplied.

## A worked cut

Lrnon's own index is built this way, and the choice is visible in what it
stores. Each lesson is split at its `##` headings, and each piece carries three
things: the lesson title, the heading it came from, and the text.

That is not decoration. The title and heading are what let a retrieved passage
be labelled honestly when it is shown to a learner as a source — and a passage
you cannot label is a passage you cannot cite.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Take a document you would want an assistant to answer from.

Cut it at its headings. Then pick the three passages you would expect to be
retrieved most often and read each one cold, out of order. Fix any that fail
the test — usually by carrying the heading down, or by pulling in the sentence
that defines the term the passage keeps using.

## Recap

Cut on the document's own structure, not on a character count, and carry
headings down into the pieces they cover. Aim for one complete idea per
passage. Then read each candidate cold: if you cannot tell what it is about,
neither can retrieval, and the passage will do more harm than good.
