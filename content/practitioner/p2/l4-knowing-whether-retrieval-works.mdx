P1·L7 argued that a prompt doing real work deserves a test. Retrieval deserves
one more, because its failures are quieter: the answer still arrives, still
reads well, and is simply built on the wrong passage.

## Twenty questions is enough

You do not need a benchmark. You need a list.

> Collect **twenty questions people actually ask**. For each, open your
> documents and write down which passage *should* be returned.

An hour's work. What it gives you is the ability to change something —
different chunking, a different threshold, a new document — and immediately
know whether you improved things or broke them.

Real questions matter more than clever ones. Ask two colleagues what they would
type. The questions you invent are shaped by knowing what the system can do,
which is exactly the bias that hides failures.

## Write the answer down first

The temptation is to run a question, look at what came back, and decide whether
it seems reasonable. That is not a test — it is an opinion formed after seeing
the result, and it will rationalise almost anything.

Deciding the expected passage **before** running it is what makes the outcome
binary. Either the right passage was in the top few or it was not.

## Two numbers worth having

**Was the right passage retrieved at all?** If it never reaches the model, the
model cannot use it. This is the number that matters most and the one people
skip.

**How many passages did you have to supply to get it?** If the right one is
consistently fourth, your ranking is weak — you are compensating with volume,
which costs money on every single request.

## The diagnostic that saves hours

When an answer is wrong, one question comes first:

> **Was the right passage retrieved?**

Two very different problems live behind that:

- **It was not retrieved.** A retrieval failure. Changing the prompt, the
  model, or the temperature will do nothing, and hours disappear into tuning
  the half that was working.
- **It was retrieved and the answer is still wrong.** A generation failure —
  now the prompt, the fencing and the refusal rules are the right place to
  look, which is L5 and L7.

Log what was retrieved for every request. Not the answer, not the question if
it may contain personal detail — just which passages were supplied. Without it
you are guessing at which half broke.

## Watch for the silent degradation

Retrieval quality drifts as content grows. A collection of fifty passages is
easy; the same questions against five thousand are not, because there are now
many near-misses competing with the right answer.

Re-run the twenty whenever the collection grows meaningfully. It takes minutes,
and it is the only way to notice a system that got worse without anything
visibly breaking.

## Try it now (10 minutes)

Write five questions and their expected passages. Run them.

If all five pass, your five questions are too easy — add the awkward ones:
something phrased in the user's vocabulary rather than the document's,
something answered in two places, and something the documents do not cover at
all. That last one is the most valuable test you will write, and L7 is about
what should happen when it fires.

## Recap

Twenty real questions with their expected passages written down in advance.
Measure whether the right passage was retrieved and how deep you had to reach.
Log what was supplied on every request, so a wrong answer can be attributed to
retrieval or to generation rather than guessed at. Re-run as the collection
grows, because that is when quality drifts silently.
