You have passages. A question arrives. Which three or four do you supply?

## Keyword matching

Score each passage by the question's words that appear in it. Simple, fast, and
better than its reputation.

Its strength is exactness. Product codes, error numbers, clause references,
surnames — nobody paraphrases `ERR_5521`, and a meaning-based search may
happily return something *about* errors instead of the one you named.

Its weakness is vocabulary. Ask *"can I send this back?"* of a policy that says
"returns" and never says "send back", and keyword matching finds nothing.

### Rare words count for more

One refinement does most of the work, and it is worth understanding rather than
importing.

On a site about AI, the word "AI" appears in nearly every passage. Matching it
tells you almost nothing. A word appearing in three passages out of nine
hundred tells you a great deal.

> **Weight each matching word by how rare it is across the collection.**

That single idea is what stops every question returning the introduction. It is
usually called IDF — inverse document frequency — and the intuition is the
whole of it: common words distinguish nothing, rare words distinguish a lot.

## Meaning-based matching

Convert each passage into a list of numbers — an *embedding* — positioned so
that texts about similar things sit near each other. Convert the question the
same way, and return the nearest passages.

Now "can I send this back" lands near "returns" without sharing a word. This is
genuinely powerful and it is why the technique took over.

Its weakness is the mirror image: it is fuzzy where you need exact. Ask for
`ERR_5521` and you may get passages about error handling generally, because in
meaning-space those are close. It also needs a model to produce the embeddings,
which is a dependency, a cost, and a thing that changes underneath you.

## The honest answer is both

Neither is a strategy. Run both, combine the rankings, and you cover each one's
blind spot with the other's strength. This is usually called *hybrid retrieval*
and it is the default worth starting from.

## Two gates, not one score

Ranking tells you which passages are *most* relevant. It does not tell you
whether the best one is relevant **at all** — a ranked list always has a top
item, even when everything in it is nonsense.

So a threshold is needed. And a single score is not enough, for a reason
measured directly on Lrnon's own index:

> Asked *"What is the capital of France?"*, the highest-scoring passage on the
> whole site scored **6.28** — higher than any genuinely in-scope question. It
> matched because E1·L6 contains the sentence *"The capital of France is ___"*
> as a next-word-prediction example, and "capital" and "France" are rare enough
> here to carry enormous weight.

Score alone was actively misleading. The fix was a second, independent gate:
what fraction of the question's distinctive words appear at all. Genuine
questions covered 50–100% of theirs; that one covered a third. **Both** gates
must pass.

The general lesson is worth more than the specific numbers: a rare word
appearing incidentally outranks a common word appearing meaningfully, so any
system relying on a single relevance number will eventually be confidently
wrong in exactly this way.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Write down five questions people actually ask about your document, in their
words rather than the document's.

For each, note whether a keyword search would find the right passage. Where it
would not, you have found the vocabulary gap that meaning-based search exists
to close — and a list of terms worth adding to the document itself, which is
often the cheaper fix.

## Recap

Keyword search is exact and vocabulary-bound; meaning-based search is flexible
and imprecise. Run both. Weight rare words heavily so common ones stop
dominating. And never trust a single relevance score to decide whether anything
was found at all — a ranked list always has a winner.
