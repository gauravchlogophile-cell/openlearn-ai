P1 was about asking well. This module is about changing what the model is
answering *from*.

Everything in Explorer assumed the model knew things. It does — a great many —
but what it knows is a compressed impression of text it saw during training,
with no index, no dates and no way to tell you which part it is drawing on.
E7·L1 named the consequence: fluency and accuracy are produced by different
machinery, and they come apart without a visible signal.

Grounding is the structural fix. Instead of asking a question and hoping, you
put the relevant passage **in the request** and ask the model to answer from
that.

## The shift, in one comparison

> *"What is our refund window?"*

The model answers from an impression of every refund policy it has ever read.
The answer will be plausible. It may even be right, by coincidence. You cannot
tell which.

> *"Here is section 4 of our returns policy. What is our refund window?
> Quote the sentence you used."*

Now the model is reading. The task has changed from recall to comprehension,
and comprehension is the thing these systems are genuinely good at.

## What this actually buys you

**Currency.** A model's training has a cutoff. Your policy changed last
Tuesday. No amount of prompting fixes that; supplying the current text does.

**Specificity.** "Our" refund window is not a fact about the world. It is a
fact about one document, and only that document contains it.

**Checkability** — the one that matters most. A grounded answer comes with
a place to look. E7·L3's two-minute method becomes possible: open the source,
read the sentence in place. An ungrounded answer offers nothing to check
against except your own memory.

## What it does not buy you

Grounding is not a truth serum, and the failure it does not fix is worth
naming early because it is the one people meet in production.

**A passage that mentions the topic is not a passage that answers the
question.** Retrieval finds text that looks relevant. Whether that text
actually contains the answer is a separate question, and nothing about
supplying it forces the model to notice the difference.

This is not hypothetical. Lrnon's own assistant retrieves from these lessons,
and asked "what is the capital of France?" it legitimately surfaces E1·L6 —
because that lesson contains the phrase *"the capital of France is ___"* as a
next-word-prediction example. The retrieval is correct. The passage does not
answer the question. Only an explicit rule to refuse when the passages fall
short, plus a check that discards an answer citing nothing, closes that gap.

You will build both in L7 and L8.

## The shape of the thing you are building

Four steps, and the rest of this module is each one in turn:

> 1. **Split** your documents into passages small enough to be useful (L2)
> 2. **Find** the few that bear on the question (L3, L4)
> 3. **Supply** them, clearly fenced, with the question (L5)
> 4. **Refuse** when they do not answer it, and prove what was used (L7, L8)

Step four is the one that gets skipped, and it is the one that decides whether
the thing you built is trustworthy or merely impressive.

## Try it now (5 minutes)

Take a question you already ask an assistant, where the answer depends on a
document you control — a policy, a spec, a set of notes.

Ask it cold. Then paste the relevant page in and ask again, adding *"quote the
sentence you used."* Compare the two answers, and notice specifically whether
the cold answer was **wrong** or merely **unverifiable**. They are different
problems, and the second is the more common one.

## Recap

Grounding replaces "what does the model know" with "what does this passage
say". It fixes currency and specificity, and — most importantly — it makes an
answer checkable. It does not make the model honest about passages that fall
short of answering, which is the failure the rest of this module is built
around.
