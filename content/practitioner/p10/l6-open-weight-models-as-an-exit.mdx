The most thorough answer to depending on a model provider is to depend on none: run a
model you control. Explorer E2·L11 introduced the open-weight and regional assistants; this
lesson asks what they mean as an exit.

The short version is that it removes one set of risks, adds another, and is a genuine option
for some projects and a distraction for most.

## What the term means

An **open-weight** model is one whose trained parameters, the weights, are published so
that anyone can download and run it. The registry describes several. Meta's Llama family is
downloadable and runnable on your own hardware. Mistral publishes both open-weight and
hosted models, with data residency options for European users. Qwen is Alibaba's
open-weight family, notable for coverage of several Asian languages that other models
handle less well. DeepSeek publishes open-weight models with strong reasoning at markedly
lower cost than the large hosted services, usable through its own API or self-hosted.

Two cautions about the word.

**Open-weight is not necessarily open source.** The weights may be downloadable under a
licence that limits who may use them, for what, at what scale, or with which conditions
attached. Licences differ between families and between versions. Read the one that applies
to what you intend.

**A model hosted by someone else is not self-hosted.** Many open-weight models are offered
through third-party hosts. That is a different arrangement with its own terms, and P8·L4
applies to it as to any provider.

## What it removes

Running the model yourself changes the picture in L2 considerably.

**Terms that can change under you.** There is no provider to revise the price, retire the
model or alter the data terms. The files you have are the files you have.

**Silent model changes.** Nothing moves an alias. You choose the version and it stays put
until you decide otherwise, which removes a whole class of failure from P7·L6.

**Regional refusal.** No service to decide your users' location is unsupported.

**Data leaving your hands.** Prompts need not go to anyone else. For a project handling
sensitive questions, or one whose learners include children in places where sending a child's
question abroad is a real objection, that is significant.

**Per-token cost.** You pay for hardware and electricity rather than by the token, which can
be far cheaper at high volume and far dearer at low volume.

## What it adds

The list of what you take on is longer, and it is where small teams stop.

**Hardware.** Models of useful size need serious compute, usually specialised processors,
bought or rented. Running a model capable enough to be worth using is not a laptop task for
most workloads.

**Operations.** You run a service: availability, scaling, restarts, monitoring, upgrades.
Everything in P7 now applies to infrastructure you own.

**Updates.** Models improve, and staying current means evaluating and moving versions
yourself.

**Security.** You are responsible for patching, access control and the model files.

**Evaluation.** You decide whether the model is good enough, with your own test set. There is
nobody to say it is.

**Safeguards.** Hosted providers add filtering, abuse monitoring and refusal behaviour. Run
it yourself and those are your responsibility, which matters when children are among the
users.

**Capability.** Open models vary and, depending on the task and the date, may lag the best
hosted ones. Measure on your own cases and do not assume.

**Licence and jurisdiction.** Some organisations and governments restrict the use of
particular providers or models. Check the rules that apply to you.

## Is it a real exit?

Be honest about scale and staffing.

**It is a real exit when** the volume is high enough to justify the cost, you already have
people who run infrastructure, the data is sensitive enough that sending it out is the
problem, or the regions you serve are poorly covered by hosted providers.

**It is mostly a distraction when** you are small, have one maintainer, and the realistic
alternative is another hosted provider with similar terms.

For most small projects, the practical position is a **hybrid**: keep the code able to point
at a model you host, as an option, without hosting one today. That preserves the choice at
the price of a configuration setting.

## Lrnon's position

The assistant's adapter can reach any server that speaks the common chat-completions shape,
by changing a base address, and its header names self-hosting as one reason. So the door is
open in the code.

Nobody has gone through it. No self-hosted model has been run behind the adapter, no
evaluation has been done on one, and no one on the project operates inference hardware. With
a single maintainer and a free-tier budget, running its own model is not a realistic near-term
exit.

That is a fair thing to say. It is an option that is preserved cheaply and not a plan, and
the distinction matters.

## Try it now (20 minutes)

For a workflow of yours, answer six questions in writing.

1. What volume do we run, and what would hosting cost at that volume?
2. Who would operate it, and what happens when they are away?
3. What is the licence of the model we would use, and does it permit our use?
4. How would we evaluate it against our test set?
5. What safeguards does the provider supply that we would now owe?
6. Is the realistic alternative another hosted provider?

If the answers make self-hosting unrealistic today, write down what would have to change,
such as volume, a second operator or a data restriction. That is a trigger worth recording.

## Recap

Open-weight models can be downloaded and run yourself, though licences vary and open-weight
is not the same as open source. Running one removes per-token provider terms, silent model
changes, regional refusals and data leaving your hands. It adds hardware, operations,
updates, security, evaluation and the safeguards a provider used to supply, which is where
small teams stop. It is a real exit at scale or with sensitive data and a distraction for a
small team, for whom the sensible position is to preserve the option in code without
hosting anything today.
