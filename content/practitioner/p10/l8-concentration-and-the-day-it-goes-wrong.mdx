The last lesson in the module draws the others together into a question about shape: how
much of your work rests on how few things?

Concentration is attractive because it is convenient. One vendor for everything means one
bill, one login and one way of working. It also means that what fails, fails all at once.

## Where concentration hides

**One vendor, many functions.** Hosting, database, sign-in and the build pipeline with the
same company. A fault or a policy change affects all of it at the same moment, and so does
a suspended account.

**One cloud underneath.** Several vendors that look independent may all run on the same
infrastructure provider. When that goes down, the apparently separate services go down
together. This is a **correlated failure**: independence you thought you had was not there.

**One account.** Everything registered to one login. Lose it and lose everything.

**One person.** The keys, the knowledge and the habit of how it fits. P7·L8 made the point
about monitoring; it holds here for the whole of the project.

**One region or one provider's goodwill.** A feature that works only where a provider
chooses to serve, as the location refusal showed.

None of these is wrong by itself. Each is a trade of resilience for simplicity, and it
should be a trade you made on purpose.

## What reducing it costs

Spreading risk is not free, and the cost is why concentration persists.

**More vendors** means more accounts, more bills, more to keep current and more places for
things to differ.

**Redundancy** means paying for something you hope never to use.

**Independence** is harder to establish than it looks. Two vendors that share a cloud do not
protect you from that cloud.

So the sensible approach is selective. Spread the risks that are catastrophic and cheap to
spread. Accept the ones that are costly to spread and tolerable if they happen.

## Cheap reductions

Several are worth doing at almost any scale.

> **Backups somewhere else.** A backup held by the same vendor as the data fails with it.
> Keep a copy under a different account, ideally a different provider.
>
> **A second administrator** on every account that matters.
>
> **Credentials in a store a second person can reach,** with a written way to recover.
>
> **Your content in a form you hold,** which a repository of plain text gives you.
>
> **A written runbook** (P7·L8) so that a capable stranger could keep it running for a few
> days.
>
> **A known fallback for the user-facing part:** a degrade path that is true.

The backups point is worth checking against your own setup. For many small projects the
answer to "where are the backups?" turns out to be "with the same vendor", which protects
against a mistake and not against losing the vendor.

## Exit triggers

An exit plan (L4) says how to leave. A trigger says **when**.

Without one, the decision happens under stress, in the middle of a failure, with the least
information and the most pressure.

Write the conditions in advance. Examples:

> A price rise above a stated figure that we cannot absorb.
> A change of terms that makes a promise to our users untrue.
> Two serious outages within a quarter.
> A retirement notice for something we depend on, with less than a set period.
> A suspension of our account that is not reversed within a set time.
> A security incident at the vendor affecting our data.

Each should have a threshold specific enough to be unambiguous and an owner who decides.
The point is not that you will leave when one occurs. It is that the *question* gets asked
on a schedule you chose, and not only when you are already in trouble.

## Review it every year

Dependencies shift. A vendor you chose for good reasons changes, you grow, your data changes,
a better option appears.

Once a year, run the whole module in an hour:

1. Redraw the dependency map (L1). What is new?
2. Check the six ways (L2). Has any happened, or become likelier?
3. Re-estimate the lock-in table (L3). Has the largest figure moved?
4. Look at the exit plan (L4). Was it rehearsed? When was the last restore?
5. Re-read the terms that matter and update the dated note (L7).
6. Check the triggers (this lesson). Is any near?

Put it in the calendar. A review that is not scheduled does not happen.

## Lrnon's concentration, said plainly

Tallied honestly, the picture has real concentration in several places.

A small number of vendors carry the hosting, build, database, sign-in and model. Several
are free-tier relationships. One person holds the accounts, the knowledge and the
recovery. The content and the schema are held in a repository, which is a genuine strength,
and the user-facing assistant degrades to something true, which is another.

What the project does not yet have, as far as this record shows: a rehearsed exit, a second
administrator on the vendor accounts, confirmation that backups sit apart from the data, a
role-based recovery path in place of accounts held under one person's own identity, written
exit triggers, and a dated note on any vendor's terms. Some of these may exist and simply
not have been recorded, which is itself the gap.

That is not unusual for a project of this size, and not a reason for alarm. It is a list
with six items on it, each cheap to start, and the value of the exercise is that they are now
written down.

## Try it now (45 minutes)

Run the annual review once, now, on one project. Work through the six steps and keep notes.

Then write three exit triggers specific enough to be unambiguous, with an owner for each,
and put the next review in the calendar a year from today.

## Recap

Concentration is convenient and makes one failure into every failure. It hides in one
vendor doing many jobs, one cloud under several vendors, one account, one person and one
provider's goodwill. Reducing it costs effort, so spread the catastrophic and cheap risks:
backups elsewhere, a second administrator, recoverable credentials, content you hold and a
runbook. Write exit triggers in advance with owners, and run the whole module as an annual
review. Lrnon's list of six missing pieces is short, cheap to start, and now written down.
