A vendor does not need to disappear to leave you worse off. Most of the damage comes from
vendors who carry on trading and change something you relied on.

There are six patterns. Each is ordinary, and each has happened in some form to this
project in a few weeks.

## 1. The price changes

A free tier shrinks, a limit drops, a per-token rate rises, a feature moves to a paid
plan. You find out when a bill arrives or a request is refused.

**Detect it:** read the pricing page on a schedule, or have someone subscribed to
announcements. Watch your own spend (P7·L4) for a sudden shift.

**Reduce the exposure:** a cap that holds (P7·L3), a second source you could move to, and
costs expressed per unit of value so you notice when a unit gets dearer.

## 2. The terms change

What the vendor may do with your data, what you may do with the service, who is liable.
Terms are revised, usually by notice to an address you may not read.

P8·L4 gave the privacy side of this: terms differ by plan, and an assurance to your users
has to be true under the terms in force today. A change of terms can turn a true
assurance false overnight.

**Detect it:** subscribe to the notices, and date-stamp what you checked and when so that a
later change is visible against a record.

## 3. What you use is retired

A model is withdrawn, an API version sunsets, a product is discontinued, an option is no
longer accepted.

This project met it twice in a few weeks. A model name that the provider's own list still
contained returned "not found" for generation, and when the configured model was changed to
a newer one, a request option the old one accepted was rejected outright. P7·L6 told both
in detail.

**Detect it:** a periodic run of a fixed test set, a version stamp in your diagnostics, and a
probe that asks the provider what it currently offers. Reading announcements helps but is
not enough, since the first sign is often a failing call.

## 4. An outage

The service is simply unavailable, for minutes or days.

**Detect it:** your own monitoring (P7·L8), and the vendor's status page, which is often
later than your users.

**Reduce the exposure:** degrade to something true (P7·L7), and know which of your
dependencies has no substitute. An outage with a fallback is an inconvenience; one without
is an incident.

## 5. You are refused

Not an outage: a decision. An account is suspended, a region is unsupported, a use is
judged to breach a policy, a payment method fails.

This is the one people discount, because it feels personal and unlikely. It is neither.
Suspensions are often automated, appeals can be slow, and a single flagged event can close
an account that something important depends on.

A small example from this project: one call failed with a message that the user's location
was not supported. It did not recur and nobody saw it for five weeks. A Worker's outgoing
request leaves from the location nearest the visitor, so whether the provider serves it can
differ by learner, with nothing in your own configuration to explain the difference.

**Reduce the exposure:** know your account's recovery path before you need it, keep a
second administrator, and do not run everything through one account.

## 6. Ownership changes

A vendor is acquired, merged, or changes direction. Pricing, priorities, support and
product direction can change with no change of name.

**Detect it:** it is in the news, but only if someone is reading. Treat an acquisition of
a key dependency as a trigger to review the rest of this list.

## What free means

A free tier deserves its own warning, because it is where the weakest commitments live.

Free usually means no promise of availability, no promise of continuity, a limit that can
change, and terms that differ from the paid ones. It is an offer, and it can be altered or
withdrawn.

That does not make it a mistake to use one. It means building on it with open eyes:

**Do not promise users more than it can hold.** An assurance resting on a free tier's
terms is only as firm as those terms (P8·L4).

**Know the cost of the upgrade,** so that if the free tier closes you know the number.

**Keep your own copy of what matters,** because a free service can end with little notice.

For reference, when checked on 4 September 2026 the project's database organisation was on
the provider's free plan, and the model provider's quota behaved like a free tier. That is a
reasonable choice for a project with no income, and it is a fact worth having written down
rather than discovered.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

Take the map from L1. For each of the six patterns, pick the dependency where it would hurt
most and write one line:

- how you would find out,
- how long that would take,
- what you would do first.

Where the first line is "we would find out when it broke", that is the gap.

## Recap

A vendor can leave you worse off without leaving: the price changes, the terms change,
what you use is retired, the service goes down, you are refused, or ownership changes.
Decide in advance how you would find out about each. Free tiers carry the weakest
commitments, so build on them knowingly and never promise users more than they can hold.
Know your account's recovery path and keep a second administrator before you need them.
