Nobody reads terms of service for pleasure, and most agreements are written to be
unreadable. That is exactly why the handful of clauses that decide whether you can recover
from a problem are worth finding in advance.

This is general information and not legal advice. For anything that matters commercially,
have someone qualified read the actual agreement. The aim here is to know what to look for.

## Eight clauses worth finding

### 1. Termination, and by whom

Can they end it? On what notice? For what reasons? An agreement that lets the vendor end
the relationship immediately, for any reason, describes a very different risk from one with
ninety days' notice and a stated cause.

### 2. Notice of change

How will you be told about changes to the terms, the price or the service, and how much
notice do you get? "We may update these terms at any time" with notice only by posting on
a page is the weak end.

### 3. Your data when it ends

What happens to your data if you leave or are removed: can you export it, in what form, for
how long afterwards, and when is it deleted? This is where an exit plan (L4) succeeds or
fails.

### 4. Suspension

On what grounds can they suspend your account, with or without warning, and is there an
appeal? Automated systems make suspensions fast and reversals slow, and a suspended account
cannot export anything.

### 5. Service levels

Is there any commitment on availability, and what is the remedy if it is missed? Often the
remedy is a small credit against future fees, and often a free tier has no commitment at all.

### 6. Liability

Almost every agreement caps what the vendor owes you if they fail. The cap is commonly tied
to what you paid, which for a free service can be nothing or very little. Know it, and
assume that a serious failure will cost you more than you can recover.

### 7. Sub-processors and where data goes

Which other companies handle your data on the vendor's behalf, and in which countries? P8·L4
covered why this matters for personal data, and the terms are where the list lives.

### 8. What they may do with what you send

The data-use terms, which P8·L4 went through for a model provider. Training, human review,
retention and the difference between plans.

## Free and paid read differently

The same vendor can offer a free tier and a paid one under quite different terms.

Free usually means weaker commitments across the board: no service level, easier
suspension, and different data use. If you depend on something, check which set of terms
you are actually under, and whether upgrading changes the answer to the questions above. It
sometimes costs little relative to the risk it removes.

## Keep a dated note

For each vendor that matters, keep a one-page record.

> **Vendor, product, and plan.**
> **Date read, and the version or URL of the terms.**
> **The answers to the eight questions,** in a line each.
> **What you do not know.**

Why dated? Terms change, and a later dispute or doubt becomes simple to settle when you can
say *"on 15 October the terms said this, and we were on this plan."* It also shows you
checked, which is worth something if anyone later asks.

Set a reminder to re-read yearly and whenever the vendor announces changes.

## Accounts and the single administrator

One more practical risk lives in the small print's neighbourhood: who the account belongs to.

A vendor account registered to one person's personal email, with that person's phone as the
recovery route and their card on file, is a dependency on that person. If they are
unreachable, leave, or lose access to the email, so does the account.

**Have a second administrator** where the vendor allows it. **Use a role-based address**
for the account and billing contact, not a person's. **Know the recovery process** before
you need it, and what it requires: sometimes a document, sometimes a long wait.

## Where Lrnon sits

Applied to the project, honestly, and with the limits of what has been read.

When checked on 4 September 2026, the database organisation was on the provider's free plan.
That is a reasonable choice and means whatever service commitments exist are those of a free
tier, which this lesson would describe as weak and which nobody on the project has read in
detail. The model provider's quota behaved like a free tier too, and its terms for that tier
are the ones P8·L4 quotes.

The project has one person holding the accounts. That is the same risk as a single
administrator, at the scale of the whole project, and the cheapest mitigations, a role-based
contact address and written recovery steps, are worth doing before they are needed.

None of the eight clauses has been recorded in a dated note for any of Lrnon's vendors. That
is the first job this lesson produces.

## Try it now (30 minutes)

Choose the vendor whose loss would hurt most. Find its terms and answer the eight questions
in a line each, noting where you could not find the answer.

Write the date and the plan at the top. Then check who owns the account, who the recovery
contact is, and whether a second administrator exists.

## Recap

This is general information, not legal advice. Find eight clauses before depending on a
vendor: termination, notice of change, your data when it ends, suspension, service levels,
liability, sub-processors, and what they may do with what you send. Free and paid tiers
usually read differently, and free means weaker commitments across the board. Keep a dated
one-page note per vendor, re-read it yearly, and make sure the account has a second
administrator, a role-based contact and a recovery path that does not run through one
person.
