P7 was about running a model-backed feature without surprises. P8 was about the data. This
module is about the third thing that goes wrong over a long enough time: the people and
companies your work depends on stop behaving the way you assumed.

Start where P8 started, with a picture.

## Draw the layers

Most people, asked what a workflow depends on, name the model provider. That is one layer
of several.

> **The model.** The provider, the specific model, and the plan you are on.
>
> **The platform.** Whatever runs the workflow: an automation tool, a hosting service.
>
> **The data.** Where it lives: a database, a file store, a spreadsheet.
>
> **Identity.** How people sign in, and who vouches for them.
>
> **Delivery.** Email, messaging, payments, anything that reaches people.
>
> **The domain.** The name people type, with its own registrar and renewal date.
>
> **The code and its supply.** The repository, the build service, and every library the
> code pulls in.
>
> **The people.** Who holds the keys, who can recover an account, who knows how it works.

Each is a dependency. The last is the one most often left off the diagram and the one that
most often fails.

## Three questions for each

For every item, answer three things, in writing.

1. **If it were unavailable for a day, what happens?**
2. **For a week?**
3. **If it were gone for good?**

The first answer is usually "some inconvenience". The second often reveals a real cost. The
third is the one people have not thought about, and it is where the planning belongs.

Be specific. "We would be affected" is not an answer. *"Sign-in would fail, so logged-in
learners could not save progress, though lessons would still load"* is.

## The layer behind the layer

You do not only depend on what you chose. Your host depends on a cloud. Your libraries
depend on other libraries, which depend on others. Your provider runs on someone's data
centres, under someone's jurisdiction.

Those are inherited risks. You cannot remove them, but you can know about them, and a
surprising amount of "unexpected" failure is the failure of something two layers down that
you never knew you relied on.

A practical limit: you cannot map everything. Map what you can name, mark what you suspect,
and revisit the map when something fails, because each failure reveals a dependency you
had not drawn.

## A map for a small project

Lrnon's own picture, from what its repository and configuration show, is useful because it
is small enough to hold in your head.

> **Model:** an outside provider's API, reached through a thin adapter.
>
> **Platform and hosting:** Cloudflare Workers, with its key-value store, and its build
> service that deploys on every push.
>
> **Data and identity:** Supabase, for the Postgres database and for sign-in.
>
> **Sign-in providers:** Google and GitHub, plus an emailed link.
>
> **Code:** a repository on GitHub, and a tree of open-source libraries.
>
> **Domain:** lrnon.org, with its own registrar and renewal.
>
> **People:** one maintainer.

Filling in the three questions is the useful exercise. If Cloudflare were unavailable for a
day, the site would be down. If the model provider were unavailable, the site would stay up
and the assistant would degrade to search and a human, which P7·L7 designed for. If the one
maintainer were unavailable, nothing would break immediately, and nothing would be fixed.

That last answer is the one that matters most.

## Rank by consequence, not by likelihood

It is tempting to worry about what seems likely. A better ordering is by what hurts.

**High consequence, low likelihood:** the host disappears. Rare, and catastrophic. Worth a
plan even though it probably will not happen.

**Moderate consequence, high likelihood:** a provider changes a model or a limit. Common,
and costly in time. Worth routine defences, which P7 covered.

**Low consequence, any likelihood:** a font service is slow. Note it and move on.

Spend attention where the consequence is high, whatever the odds.

## Try it now (20 minutes)

Draw the layers for one workflow you run, using the eight headings above. For each
dependency write the name, the plan or tier you are on, and the answers to the three
questions.

Then circle every dependency where "gone for good" produces a blank. Those are the subject
of L4.

## Recap

Dependency risk starts with a list, and the list has layers: the model, the platform, the
data, identity, delivery, the domain, the code and its supply, and the people. For each,
write what happens if it is gone for a day, a week and for good, specifically. You inherit
the dependencies of your dependencies, and each failure reveals one you had not drawn. Rank
by consequence and not likelihood. For Lrnon, the sharpest answer on the page is the
last row: one person.
