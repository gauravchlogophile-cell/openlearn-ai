You cannot manage what a system cost, or why it failed, if you did not write it
down at the time. This lesson is about what to write, where, and for how long.

It is also the lesson where this project's own record is least flattering, because
the mistakes were instructive.

## The record

For every model call, one row. The fields that earn their place:

> **When** it was reserved and when it was settled.
> **Which** provider and model.
> **How many** tokens went in and came out.
> **Whether** it succeeded.
> **If not, why.**

That is Lrnon's spend table, and it is small on purpose. It is enough to answer the
questions that come up: what did Tuesday cost, which model was in use when it
changed, how long do calls take, how often do they fail and for what reason.

What is deliberately **not** in it is the question. P3·L8 gave the reason: a record
of what people asked an assistant is a record of what they were worried about, and
on a learning site that includes children. The shape of a call supports almost every
investigation, and a transcript is a second exposure you created. Note too that the
reason column holds our own words about a status code, never the provider's body,
which can quote the request back.

## The reason, in the same place

The failure column was added late, and the occasion is the best argument for it.

An injection test run kept losing about ten of its twenty-seven cases to an error
that said only *provider*. The route had been written to return a reason, the
console to display it, and the deployed code had been checked and did contain both.
The reason still never arrived, because every path to it ran through a person copying
text from a screen, and detail was lost at each copy.

Four rounds went on guessing. The cause was then added to the same row that recorded
the failure, and read straight out of the database. It said the provider had
refused for exceeding its quota, and the per-minute counts showed it at once.

> A diagnostic that depends on someone relaying it is not a diagnostic.

Put the reason where it can be queried, in the record of the failure itself.

## A window long enough to find things

The second lesson is about time.

The console summarised the previous seven days of calls, with a count of how many
failed. On 9 September one call failed with a message saying the provider did not
serve the region the request came from. Nobody saw it. The count read *one*, and the
reason was not shown anywhere. By the time anyone looked, five weeks later, the
seven-day window had dropped it entirely.

Two separate gaps made that possible.

**The count named nothing.** A number of failures says that something went wrong and
nothing about what. Group failures by reason and show the reasons.

**The window was shorter than the interval between looks.** If you check weekly,
evidence needs to last longer than a week, or whatever happened in the middle goes
unseen. The failures panel now covers 180 days.

The general rule: **make the window longer than the gap between looks, by a wide
margin.**

## Cost per outcome

Raw totals are the beginning. The useful figures divide.

> Cost per answered question.
> Tokens per call, over time, and whether the trend is up.
> Failure rate, and which reason dominates.
> Share of calls refused before reaching the model.

The trend is where things show. Tokens per call creeping up usually means context is
growing. A rising failure rate with one reason dominant points at a cause. A falling
share of early refusals suggests something has stopped catching what it used to.

A caution about interpretation. A failed call is not necessarily free. A request that
is rejected before generating anything is normally not billed, but one that times out
after the provider has started may be. Check your provider's rules rather than
assuming, and look at the bill as well as your own table.

## Read it, sometimes

A record nobody reads is a record that failed.

Set a time, weekly is plenty at small scale, and look at three things: spend against
the cap, the list of distinct failure reasons, and any trend in tokens per call. Ten
minutes. Most weeks nothing will be wrong, and that is the point of the habit: the
week something is wrong, you are already looking.

## What the record cannot show

Be honest about the limits.

Lrnon's table cannot tell you whether an answer was **good**. It records that a call
was made, used some tokens, and succeeded. Whether the answer was right is a separate
measurement, which is what the review buttons and readiness gates exist for, and
those have recorded nothing yet. The cost record and the quality record are different
things, and a healthy-looking spend table says nothing about the second.

A system can be cheap, fast, reliable and wrong. Measure all four.

## Try it now (12 minutes)

For a feature you run, check three things.

1. **What is recorded per call?** Is there a reason beside each failure?
2. **How long does the record last,** and how often do you look? Is the first longer
   than the second by a wide margin?
3. **Does anything in it contain what a person asked?** If so, ask whether the
   investigation really needs it.

Then do the weekly look once, now, and write down what you would not have been able
to answer.

## Recap

Record one row per model call with when, which model, tokens in and out, whether it
succeeded and, if not, why, and leave out the question itself. Keep the reason in the
same record as the failure, because a diagnostic that depends on someone relaying it
loses detail. Make the retention window much longer than the gap between your looks,
group failures by reason, and divide totals into cost per outcome. Look weekly, and
remember the cost record says nothing about whether the answers were good.
