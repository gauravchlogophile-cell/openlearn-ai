Everyone intends to have a spending limit. Far fewer have one that holds under
pressure, because most limits are checked in a way that fails exactly when it is
needed.

## The race

Picture the obvious implementation. A request arrives, reads today's total, sees it
is under the limit, makes the call, and adds its cost to the total.

Now send twenty requests at once.

Each reads the total, finds it under the limit, and proceeds. By the time any has
written its cost back, all twenty have already passed the check. The limit was never
consulted in a way that could stop them, because no request saw another's.

> **Check, then act, then record** is a race. The check and the record have to be
> one step.

That is the whole difference between a soft cap and a hard one. A soft cap is
approximately right on a quiet day and wrong precisely under load, which is when
an attacker, a bug or a viral moment produces the load.

## A soft cap, honestly described

Lrnon has one of each, and they are described in the code as what they are.

The per-visitor limiter reads a count from a key-value store, compares it, and
writes it back. That store is neither atomic nor immediately consistent across
locations, so two requests can both read five and both write six, and a client
spreading requests across locations can see stale counts for several seconds.

The code says so plainly: it bounds nuisance, not money. It is there to keep one
person from hammering the endpoint, and it does that job well enough. It was never
meant to protect the bill, and its own comment warns against letting it quietly
become the thing that does.

## A hard cap

The control that protects the money lives in the database, and it works differently.

Before any model call, the server asks the database to **reserve** the worst-case
cost. One statement, under a row lock, checks whether the day's total plus this
reservation would exceed the cap, and if not, records the reservation. Because the
check and the record are the same operation, and the lock admits one request at a
time, twenty simultaneous requests are processed one after another. The twenty-first
that would breach the limit is refused.

Afterwards the reservation is **settled**: corrected down to what was actually used.

Three properties make it hold.

**One place.** The check and the increment cannot be separated, so there is no gap
for a second request to slip through.

**Worst case first.** The reservation is the maximum the call could use, not an
estimate. Reserving the estimate afterwards is the original race again.

**Settling downward.** Reserving the maximum would over-count, so it is corrected
once the real figure is known. A failure to settle over-counts the day, which is the
safe direction to be wrong in.

## Several caps, for different failures

One limit rarely covers it. Lrnon has three, and each catches something the others
do not.

> **Per call.** The most any single call may use. It bounds the worst case that the
> reservation takes, and stops one enormous request from consuming a day.
>
> **Per day, in tokens.** The money cap. The one that actually binds, as L1 showed.
>
> **Per day, in calls.** A guard against a flood of tiny requests, which would stay
> under the token cap and still be a problem.

The same logic applies at the provider. Most let you set a spending limit or an
alert on your account. Set one. It is the second line, and unlike yours it still
works if your own code has a bug.

## Fail closed

The question that distinguishes a real cap is what it does when it cannot count.

If the database is unreachable, the service key is missing, or the counter returns an
error, there are two choices. Allow the call, on the reasoning that availability
matters. Or refuse it.

For money the answer is to refuse. A cap that lets everything through when its
counter is unreachable works only when nothing is wrong, and it is the failures that
produce surprise bills. Lrnon's reservation returns *not allowed* in every one of
those cases, and says why to a verified staff member, not to the learner.

The reasoning does not carry over to everything. A feature that merely displays
something can reasonably fail open. Spending money, sending messages and changing
records should fail closed. Decide for each, deliberately, and write the decision
down.

## Tell someone

There is a gap in the design above, and it is worth stating because it is typical.

A cap stops spending. It does not tell anyone. When Lrnon's daily limit is reached,
the assistant tells learners it is off and the console shows the spend table, and
nobody is notified. Whoever next looks will find it, which might be tomorrow.

Add an alert at a fraction of the cap, such as eighty percent, sent somewhere a
person reads. A cap that stops the harm and leaves the owner to find out later is
half a control.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

For a feature you run, answer five questions.

1. **Where is the check, and where is the increment?** If they are different
   operations, there is a race.
2. **Is the amount reserved before the call, as a maximum?**
3. **What happens when the counter cannot be read?**
4. **Is there a second cap at the provider?**
5. **Who is told when it is nearly reached?**

The honest answers are usually no, no, it goes ahead, no, and nobody. Each is a
small piece of work and together they are the difference between a limit and a
hope.

## Recap

A cap that checks then acts then records is a race, and a soft cap fails exactly
under load. A hard cap makes the check and the increment one operation under a lock,
reserves the worst case before the call and settles downward afterwards. Use several
caps, per call, per day in tokens and per day in calls, and set a second limit at the
provider. Fail closed when the counter cannot be read, and add an alert before the
limit, because a cap that stops spending without telling anyone is half a control.
