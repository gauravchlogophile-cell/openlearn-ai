Lessons 1 to 4 were about limits you set on yourself. This one is about limits that
are set on you, which arrive without warning, vary by account, and tell you very
little when you hit them.

## What a provider limits

Several things at once, and they are different.

> **Requests per minute.** How often you may call.
> **Tokens per minute.** How much you may send and receive in that time.
> **Requests or tokens per day.** A longer window with its own ceiling.
> **Concurrent requests.** How many may be in flight together.

Hitting any one produces a refusal, usually an HTTP 429. The status tells you that
you are over a limit and not which one, and the message wording varies.

This matters because the right response differs. A per-minute limit clears in a
minute. A daily one does not clear until tomorrow. Retrying a daily-quota refusal
every twenty seconds spends effort and achieves nothing.

## Measure the limit

The published figure is a starting point. It may describe a different tier from yours,
may have changed since the page was written, or may not describe the limit that
actually binds first.

So measure it, as Lrnon did without intending to.

A test sent 27 model calls, spaced 4.5 seconds apart. A third of them failed with the
provider's quota message, and the failures moved around from run to run, which first
made them look like something about specific cases. Reading the counts per minute
from the database across several runs settled it. In the three runs that matter, the
pattern was the same: **eight calls succeeded in a minute, then everything else that
minute was refused.** The failing cases were just whichever fell after the eighth.

The measured figure was lower than the spacing allowed. 4.5 seconds is thirteen calls
a minute against a limit of about eight to ten. Pacing was then set at 8 seconds, a
little under the limit, and the run completed.

The method generalises: send a run, count successes per minute, repeat. A number that
stays the same is the limit.

The limit also belongs to the account tier at that moment. A free tier is usually much
tighter than a paid one, and moving up changes the figure. Note the date you
measured, and re-measure when something changes.

## Pace to it

Stay under the limit rather than bumping against it.

**Space the calls.** Convert the limit into a gap: eight a minute is one every 7.5
seconds, so use a little more than that.

**Cap concurrency.** Several requests in flight at once can breach a per-minute limit
all together even if the average is fine.

**Do not run two loops.** The request counts suggested this happened here, at eleven
to sixteen a minute where one loop sends under eight. A wait of a full minute looks
like a freeze, so a person presses the button again, and now two loops compete for
the same quota. Both slow down and both fail more. A run that looks stuck during a deliberate
wait should say so on screen.

## Retry once, and wait the right time

Retries are where well-meant code makes things worse.

**Wait for the window, not for a short pause.** If the limit is per minute, a
twenty-second retry will fail again. A rate-limit refusal should wait roughly a full
minute. Other failures can retry sooner, because waiting a minute for a wrong model
name helps nobody.

**Retry once.** Not forever. A call that fails twice is telling you something, and
a loop that retries indefinitely turns a quota problem into a runaway.

**Back off with some randomness** if many callers share a limit. Otherwise they all
retry at the same instant and collide again. At small scale this rarely matters; at
larger scale it is the difference between recovering and failing in waves.

## Make the run resumable

A long run against a tight limit will, sooner or later, be interrupted. By a quota,
a timeout, a closed tab, a second press of a button.

If an interrupted run loses everything, you must finish it in one go, which a tight
limit may not allow. So design runs to **accumulate**: keep the results already
obtained, and let a re-run cover only the gaps.

Lrnon's test console does exactly that. Results are merged rather than reset, and a
button beneath the table re-runs only the cases that did not complete. Seven cases
fit comfortably inside one window where twenty-seven do not. A corpus finished over
two sittings is not a worse result than one finished at once.

## When the limit is the product

For a learner-facing feature there is a harder question than pacing: what happens
when a *user's* request hits the limit?

Lrnon's per-visitor limiter and daily caps exist partly for this, and the answer
shown to the learner is the same plain message whatever the reason: the assistant is
off for now, search still works, and a person answers feedback within two working
days. The operator-facing diagnostic says which limit it was. Learners need to know
what to do next, and not which internal ceiling was reached.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

If you have anything that calls a provider repeatedly, measure its limit. Run it
three times, count successes in each minute, and write down the stable number with
today's date and your tier.

Then check four things: does your code space calls to stay under it, does a rate-limit
refusal wait for the window, does it retry once and no more, and can an interrupted
run resume without redoing completed work?

## Recap

Providers limit requests and tokens per minute and per day, and a 429 does not say
which. Measure the real limit by counting successes per minute across runs, note the
tier and the date, and pace a little under it. Wait for the whole window before
retrying a rate limit, retry once, and avoid starting a second loop while the first
waits. Make long runs resumable so an interruption costs only the gaps, and give the
people using the feature a plain message that tells them what to do next.
