Explorer E11 explained what a token is and why the bill moved. P6·L2 showed that a
loop multiplies it. This module is about running a model-backed thing in
production without the bill, the limits, or the provider's behaviour surprising
you.

It starts with arithmetic, because most surprises are arithmetic nobody did.

## Three numbers

> **Tokens per call.** Everything sent in, plus everything that comes back.
>
> **Calls per day.** How often it runs.
>
> **Price per token.** Set by the provider, usually different for input and
> output, and liable to change.

Multiply them and you have a daily figure. The mistake is not getting the
multiplication wrong. It is skipping it, and judging the cost from how the thing
feels in a demonstration, where the questions are short and the volume is one.

Do not look up a price from memory or from an old article. Prices move, and the
current ones are on the provider's own page. What matters here is the structure of
the estimate, which does not change.

## Estimate two cases

**Typical.** The question people usually ask, the usual amount of context, the usual
number of calls. This is what you will see most days.

**Worst.** The longest question you allow, the largest context you attach, the
output at its ceiling, and the highest volume you might plausibly see. Not the
theoretical maximum of a malicious user, which is a separate problem for the next
lesson, but the bad day of an ordinary one.

A budget has to hold on the worst case. A system that is affordable on average and
ruinous on a busy day is not affordable.

## Measure rather than guess

Once anything is running, replace the estimate with a measurement.

Lrnon's assistant ran a 27-case test against the real model and recorded what it
used: **22,037 tokens across the 27 calls, about 800 per call.** That is a small
number, and it is a real one, and it changes what the budget means.

The daily limits are 200,000 tokens and 500 calls. At about 800 tokens a call, the
token limit is reached after roughly 250 calls, long before the call limit. So the
call cap, which looks like the protection, is not the one that will bind. The token
cap is.

You only learn that by dividing. Two limits sit side by side in the settings and
nothing tells you which one matters.

## Output costs more, and thinking counts

Two details change the estimate.

**Output is usually the dearer side.** Providers commonly charge more per token for
what the model writes than for what it reads. A feature that reads a lot and
writes a little costs less than its token count suggests; one that writes at length
costs more.

**Reasoning tokens usually bill as output.** A model that thinks before answering
spends tokens doing so, and those are typically charged. Where it is on, a one-line
answer can cost many times its visible length.

Lrnon asks for reasoning to be off, because the task is reading four passages and
answering in a few sentences. The current model rejected that option, so the request
falls back to being sent without it and the model's own default applies. Answers
measured about 50 tokens, which suggests little reasoning, but that is a
measurement and not a setting.

It also exposed a bug worth owning. The provider reports reasoning tokens in a field
separate from the answer, and Lrnon was reading only the answer. Had the model
reasoned, the recorded spend would have been lower than the bill, and the daily cap is
enforced against the recorded figure. It now adds the two. Check how your provider
reports and counts both, on its own documentation, and re-check when the model
changes.

## Per unit of value, not per month

A flat figure at the end of a month tells you what was spent and not whether it was
spent well.

Divide by something that matters. Cost per answered question. Cost per record
processed. Cost per ticket resolved. A feature at a hundred pounds a month that
resolves three tickets is expensive; one at a hundred pounds that resolves three
thousand is not, and the monthly figures are identical.

It also tells you how cost scales. If the cost per unit is stable, doubling usage
doubles the bill, and you can plan. If it climbs with usage, because context grows
or loops lengthen, you have a different problem and the monthly figure was hiding
it.

## Try it now (12 minutes)

Take a feature you run or plan. Write down:

1. Tokens in, tokens out, per call, typical and worst.
2. Calls per day, typical and busy.
3. The provider's current prices, from its own page, with today's date.

Multiply out both cases. Then ask which of your limits will be reached first, and
whether you have a number for it or only a feeling.

If you can run it, measure twenty real calls and replace your estimate with the
result. The gap between the two is the most useful thing you will learn this week.

## Recap

A cost estimate is tokens per call, calls per day and the current price, worked out
for a typical day and a worst one, and the budget has to hold on the worst. Measure
once anything runs, because the measurement can change which limit matters: Lrnon's
call cap is not the one that binds, its token cap is. Output usually costs more
than input and reasoning tokens usually count as output. Judge cost per unit of
value rather than per month, since that is the number that shows how it scales.
