The last lesson in this module draws the earlier ones together into something a
person can actually do. Monitoring that nobody reads is decoration, and the useful
version is smaller than most dashboards.

## A short list

Pick signals by a single test: **will someone act on it?** A figure that never
prompts a decision is clutter, and clutter buries the signal that matters.

For a model-backed feature, a short list covers nearly everything:

> **Spend against the cap.** Is today going to hit the limit, and is the trend up?
>
> **Failure reasons.** Grouped, newest first, over a long window.
>
> **Latency.** How long calls take, and whether that is drifting.
>
> **Refusals before the model.** The share of requests turned away early, which
> should be stable.
>
> **Quality.** Whatever tells you whether the answers are good.

Five. Resist adding a sixth because it is easy to collect.

## Where Lrnon actually stands

Measured against that list, honestly.

**Spend:** shown in the console, seven days of it. No alert before the cap.

**Failure reasons:** shown, grouped, 180 days. Added after a failure went unseen for
five weeks.

**Latency:** each settled call has a reserved and a settled time, so it can be worked
out, but nothing displays or tracks it.

**Refusals before the model:** not kept anywhere that can be queried, though some
reach the Worker's log. If a rule started refusing everything, nothing in the console
would show it.

**Quality:** the review buttons exist and had recorded nothing when this was written.
The readiness gates that depend on them read zero.

So one of five signals is in good shape, two are partial, and two are absent or empty.
That is a fair account of a project that built the mechanism before the habit, and
it is better said than glossed.

## A weekly ten minutes

Cadence matters more than coverage. A modest look every week beats a perfect dashboard
checked on the day something has already gone wrong.

Put it in the calendar. Fixed time, ten minutes, the same order every week:

1. Spend against the cap, and the direction.
2. Distinct failure reasons: anything new?
3. Anything in the quality record.
4. Any announcement from the provider.

Most weeks nothing will be wrong. That is the design. The week something is, you are
already looking, and you know what normal looks like because you have seen it fifty
times.

## Alerts are for the few things that cannot wait

An alert interrupts a person. Spend it carefully.

**Alert on what needs action soon:** the cap nearly reached, the failure rate suddenly
high, the heartbeat from P5·L5 missed.

**Do not alert on what can wait for Tuesday:** a slightly higher latency, a single
isolated failure, a trend.

Alerts that fire often and mean little teach people to ignore them. P5·L6 made this
argument about approvals, and it applies here without change: an alert that has
become furniture protects nothing, while looking like protection.

Send them somewhere that a human reads and that is not their general inbox.

## A runbook

The runbook is the thing most likely to be missing, and the one that matters most on a
bad day.

For each symptom, write what to check first, in order, and what each result means.
Lrnon's assistant, for example:

> **Symptom: Sky says it is off.**
> 1. Look at the stage in the console. Is it off, or narrowed from the ceiling?
> 2. Run the self-test. Read the diagnostic, which names the stage that stopped it.
> 3. If it says provider, read the failure reason. A 404 means the model name; a 429
>    means a quota; a 400 naming a location means the region.
> 4. If it says budget, read why: no key, no database, or the cap reached.
> 5. Press the model-list button to see what the provider currently offers.

That is useful because it was built from real incidents, and every step exists
because it once took a long time to find. The tools it names, the self-test and the
model probe, were built for exactly this.

Write it calm. Read it in a hurry. Make it short enough that someone will follow it
rather than skip to guessing.

## One person is a risk

Lrnon is run by one person, and the project says so openly.

For monitoring, that has a specific meaning. If that person is away, ill, or simply
not looking, nothing else is: no second pair of eyes, no one to notice the cap was
hit on Thursday.

There is no complete answer to that at this size, only reductions.

**Write things down** so that a capable stranger could keep it running for a few days:
the runbook, the commands, where the secrets live and who may touch them.

**Make failure safe by default.** Caps that refuse, a degrade path that is true, a
kill switch that works. A system that fails closed while unattended is a system you
can leave alone.

**Be honest about response time.** The promise that a person answers within two
working days is a statement about staffing, and it is the right size for the staffing
that exists.

## Try it now (15 minutes)

Write the five signals for something you run, and mark each: good, partial, or absent.

Then write one runbook entry, for the failure you fear most, as steps someone else
could follow. Hand it to a colleague and ask them to say where they would get stuck.

Finally put the weekly look in the calendar before you close this page.

## Recap

Watch a short list of signals that someone will act on: spend against the cap, failure
reasons over a long window, latency, early refusals and quality. Look weekly for ten
minutes at a fixed time, reserve alerts for the few things that cannot wait, and write
a runbook of ordered steps per symptom that a stranger could follow. Be candid about
where you stand: Lrnon has one signal in good shape and two empty. With one operator,
the mitigation is writing things down and making failure safe by default.
