The last lesson of the Explorer track.

E8·L8 asked you to write a code of conduct for your work. This is the same
exercise for the questions that arrive when you are frightened, rushed, or
awake at three in the morning — which is to say, in the state where every rule
you have is hardest to follow.

That is precisely why they get written now.

## Six rules

Adopt, adapt, or reject deliberately. Write your version down.

**1. I will ask how things work, not what to do about my case.**
L1's line. Educational questions are safe and genuinely useful. Questions that
decide your specific situation need someone accountable.

**2. I will never act on a number, citation, dose or deadline from an
assistant.**
Concept from AI, figure from the official source. This one rule prevents most of
what goes seriously wrong in this module — L4's stale rates, L5's fabricated
citations, L3's confident diagnosis.

**3. I will state my jurisdiction, and distrust any answer that ignores it.**
L5's rule, and it applies to money and benefits as much as law.

**4. When I notice the spiral, I stop.**
L3. More frightened at question four than question one means the path only had
alarming answers on it. Stopping is the skill.

**5. Manufactured urgency means slow down, not hurry up.**
L6. Anything demanding a decision faster than I can check it gets more time
asked for, not a faster answer found.

**6. I will use it to prepare for people, not instead of them.**
L2 and L7. Organise the history, write the questions, understand the answers.
That is where the genuine value in this whole module sits.

## Know your own state

The rules fail in predictable conditions. Learn to notice yours.

**Frightened.** You will keep asking, and the answers will keep being alarming.

**Rushed.** You will take the first plausible answer without checking.

**Wanting a particular answer.** E7·L7's asymmetric scrutiny, at its most
expensive — hoping the lump is nothing, hoping the contract is fine, hoping you
can afford it.

**Alone at night.** Everything above, compounded, with no one to sanity-check
you.

If two or more apply, that is the moment to stop and either wait for morning or
call a line staffed by a human. Not because the technology got worse — because
you did, temporarily, and everyone does.

## What Explorer covered

Fourteen modules. What AI is and is not. The assistants and how to choose. How
to ask. Where it fits in a working day. Documents that hold up. Media and its
ethics. Verifying claims and resisting manipulation. Your own code of conduct.
Using it when reading, writing, seeing or hearing is hard. Working in your own
language. What it costs. The AI you never chose. How to actually learn. And now
the questions where being wrong hurts most.

The thread running through all of it is not enthusiasm and not suspicion. It is
that these tools are genuinely useful and specifically limited, and knowing
which is which is the entire skill.

If you continue, **Practitioner** builds things with them. If you stop here, you
have what you need — and the rules you just wrote are the part that keeps
mattering after the details fade.

## Try it now (8 minutes)

Write your six rules. Your words, one page.

Then send the three numbers from L6 to someone who might need them, and look up
one free service from L7 in your area.

Rules on paper, numbers in your phone, one service found. That is a good place
to finish.

## Recap

Six rules, written before you need them: ask how things work rather than what to
do; never act on a number, citation, dose or deadline without the official
source; state your jurisdiction; stop when you notice the spiral; treat
manufactured urgency as a reason to slow down; and use it to prepare for people
rather than instead of them. Then learn the states in which you will break them.
That completes E14, and the Explorer track.
