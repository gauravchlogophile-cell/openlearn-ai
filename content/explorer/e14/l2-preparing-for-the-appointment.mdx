If you take one practical use from this module, take this one. It is genuinely
valuable, it carries almost no risk, and almost nobody does it.

## The problem it solves

You get ten minutes. Possibly seven.

In that time you have to convey a history you have been living with for months,
in an order that makes sense, while anxious — and then absorb an explanation
containing several words you have never heard.

Most people leave having forgotten to mention the thing that mattered, and
unable to recall half of what was said. That is not a failure of intelligence.
It is what happens to anybody under those conditions.

Preparation fixes a real amount of it.

## Before: organise what you know

> *I have an appointment about [problem]. Help me organise what to tell them.*
>
> *Ask me questions one at a time to draw out the history — when it started,
> how it has changed, what makes it better or worse, what I have already tried,
> what else is going on, what I am taking.*
>
> *Then produce a short summary I can read out or hand over, in plain language,
> in the order a clinician would find most useful.*

Being **interviewed** is the important part. Left to write it yourself you
produce a narrative organised by what worries you. The questions surface the
things you would not have thought to say — timing, what changed, what you tried
— which are frequently what the clinician most needs.

## Before: write the questions down

Separately, and just as valuable:

> *What questions should I ask so I leave understanding my situation? Include
> ones people commonly forget.*

Then edit it to your own three or four. A list of fifteen does not survive
contact with a short appointment.

Take it in on paper or on your phone. Asking your questions from a list is not
rude — most clinicians would rather you did, because it is faster than the
conversation where you remember on the way out.

## After: understand what you were told

Equally strong, and less obvious.

> *I was told [what you remember]. Explain what each term means in plain
> language. Tell me what questions I should have asked, so I can ask them next
> time or in a message.*

This is E13·L2's explanation technique applied to your own care. You are not
second-guessing the clinician; you are understanding them, which is what makes
the next appointment better.

If the explanation you get conflicts with what you were told, **the clinician
saw you and the model did not**. The right move is to go back and ask, not to
form a private conclusion.

## What not to bring

One clear rule.

Do not arrive with a diagnosis you reached with an assistant, presented as a
conclusion. It is unreliable (L3), and it changes the conversation into a
negotiation about your theory rather than an examination of your symptoms.

If a possibility genuinely worries you, say it as a worry: *"I read about X and
I have been anxious about it — is that something you would consider?"* That is
useful information about you and it keeps them doing the diagnosing.

## This applies beyond health

The same shape works for a solicitor, an accountant, a benefits adviser, a
tenancy officer. Expensive or scarce time, jargon, and a history you need to
convey efficiently.

Prepare the facts, write the questions, understand the answers afterwards. It
makes scarce professional time go further, which matters most for the people who
have the least of it.

## Try it now (7 minutes)

Take a real appointment coming up, or one you had recently.

Do the interview version — let it ask you one question at a time. Notice which
question surfaced something you would not have mentioned. That is the value of
this lesson in one observation.

## Recap

Use an assistant before and after an appointment, never instead of one. Be
interviewed to organise your history, because questions surface what you would
not have thought to say. Write three or four questions and take them in. Use it
afterwards to understand the terms and work out what to ask next time. Never
arrive with a conclusion — bring a worry instead.
