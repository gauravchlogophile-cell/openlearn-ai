Getting words onto a page fails in as many ways as reading does: dyslexia and
dysgraphia, aphasia after a stroke, tremor or pain that makes typing costly,
fatigue, and the ordinary experience of knowing exactly what you mean and being
unable to start.

The useful distinction in this lesson is between **help with the mechanics** and
**someone else writing for you**. Both have their place. Confusing them is how
people end up with text that is correct and not theirs.

## Dictate, then tidy

The most effective pattern here, and the one worth trying first.

Speak the thing. Messily, out of order, with false starts — dictation into your
phone, your computer, or a voice note. Then:

> *Below is something I dictated. Fix the spelling, grammar and punctuation.
> Remove the false starts and repetitions.*
>
> *Do not change my words where they are already correct. Do not reorder my
> points. Do not make it more formal.*

This works because it separates two things that normally happen together:
having the thought, and producing correct text. If the second is where your
difficulty is, splitting them removes the barrier while leaving the content
entirely yours.

The three prohibitions matter. Without them you will get something fluent,
reordered and formal — E5·L2's generic voice, arriving where you least want it.

## Tidying versus rewriting

Two different requests, and it is worth knowing which you asked for.

**Tidying** — spelling, grammar, punctuation, obvious slips. Your words, made
correct. Nothing about the meaning changes.

**Rewriting** — restructured, rephrased, "improved". Someone else's words
carrying your meaning.

For a message to a colleague, tidying. For a formal complaint where the
conventions matter and you do not know them, rewriting may genuinely be what
you want — but ask for it deliberately, and read the result as E5·L3 suggests:
does this still say what I meant?

## Getting started when you cannot

Different problem, different tool. The blank page is not a spelling difficulty.

Two things that work:

**Talk to it first.** Ask the assistant to interview you. *"Ask me one question
at a time about what I need to write."* Answering questions is far easier than
generating from nothing, and the answers are your material.

**Ask for the shape, not the words.** E5·L1's outline technique. Knowing there
are four things to say, in order, is often all that was missing.

Both leave you writing the content. That matters for the next section.

## This does not change authorship

Worth saying plainly, because people worry about it and the worry stops them
using something that would help.

E8·L1's test is whether you can explain and defend every significant choice.
Somebody who dictated their argument, made every decision about what to say,
and used a tool to fix spelling has met that test completely. The mechanics of
transcription are not the authorship.

This is no different in kind from a spellchecker, a scribe, or a colleague
proofreading — none of which anybody thinks changes who wrote something.

Where it *does* change is if you asked for the argument itself and adopted it.
That is the line, and it is about the thinking, not the typing.

**On declared work**: if you are being assessed, ask about the specific tools
you use. Most institutions distinguish assistive use from substitution, and
many have formal provision for it — but the person to confirm that is them, not
you guessing.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Dictate something you need to write — two minutes, unedited, out loud.

Run the tidy prompt with all three prohibitions. Compare it to what you said.
If it changed your meaning or your tone anywhere, name that specifically in the
prompt next time.

## Recap

Separate help with mechanics from someone else writing. Dictate messily and ask
for spelling, grammar and punctuation only — explicitly forbidding rewording,
reordering and added formality. For a blank page, be interviewed or ask for the
shape. None of this changes authorship, because the test is whether you can
defend the choices, and the choices stayed yours.
