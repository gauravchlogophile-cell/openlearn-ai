Some communication difficulty is not about producing words. It is about the
exchange — knowing what was meant, judging how something will land, or getting
past the fact that a particular message has been sitting undrafted for eleven
days.

Autistic people, socially anxious people, people in an unfamiliar culture or
workplace, people recovering from a stroke, and people dealing with someone
difficult all meet versions of this.

## Rehearsal

The strongest use here, and underused.

> *I need to ask my manager to change my hours. Play them. They are busy and
> tend to say no first. Let me practise — one reply at a time, and stay in
> character.*

You get to have the conversation before you have it. You find out that your
opening is too apologetic, that you have no answer to the obvious objection, and
that the thing you were dreading takes four sentences.

Two things make this work better:

**Ask for the hard version.** *"Be more resistant than you think is likely."*
Practising against an easy version teaches you nothing.

**Ask what they might be thinking.** After a run, *"What was that person's
actual concern?"* frequently surfaces something you can address directly.

## Decoding, with a warning attached

Asking what a message *meant* is genuinely useful and needs one caution stated
plainly.

> *Here is an email. What is this person actually asking for? Is there anything
> implied that is not stated? Give me two or three possible readings, not one.*

Ask for **several readings**. A single confident interpretation is the failure
mode here: it sounds authoritative, it is a guess, and acting on the wrong one
has real consequences.

What you are getting is *how these words commonly land* — which is exactly the
useful thing when the conventions are the barrier. It is not access to what
somebody meant. Nobody has that but them.

When it matters, the answer is still to ask the person. "Just to check I have
understood — you want X by Friday?" is a short sentence that resolves what no
amount of analysis can.

## Drafting the message you are avoiding

The eleven-day-old undrafted message usually has an emotional obstacle, not a
linguistic one.

Two prompts that work:

> *I need to tell a client the project is late. I have been putting it off.
> Give me three openings: direct, apologetic, and neutral. One sentence each.*

Choosing between three concrete options is much easier than generating from
nothing — the same principle as E9·L6's first step.

> *Here is my draft. I am angry, and I do not want to send angry. What in this
> reads as anger? Do not rewrite it.*

Naming it rather than replacing it keeps the message yours and teaches you
something for next time.

## Keep your own register

The specific risk in this lesson.

Take the polished version wholesale and you have set an expectation. The reply
comes back pitched to a person who writes like that, and now you are sustaining
a voice that is not yours — in a conversation you already found difficult.

So: use it to find what to say, then say it your way. Shorter and plainer in
your own words beats fluent and borrowed, and it is what you will be able to
keep up when the conversation continues in person.

## What this is not

This is not therapy, and it is not a substitute for speech and language
therapy, which is a clinical discipline with actual expertise in exactly this.

Nor is it a diagnostic tool. An assistant asked whether you might be autistic
will produce something confident and unfounded, and E7 applies with full force
to anything a model says about you.

For AAC users, dedicated communication systems remain the tools built for the
job. Some now include AI-assisted prediction, and that is worth exploring
within a system that already works for you rather than in place of it.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Pick a conversation you are avoiding. Rehearse it — ask for a harder version of
the other person than you expect.

Notice which of your sentences fell apart under pressure. That is what to
rewrite, in your own words, before the real thing.

## Recap

Rehearse against a harder version than you expect, and ask what the other
person's real concern was. When decoding a message, demand several readings and
remember you are learning how words commonly land, not what somebody meant —
so ask them when it matters. Use it to find what to say, then say it in your own
register, because that is the one you can sustain.
