Some difficulties are not about words at all. Knowing exactly what needs doing
and being unable to begin. Holding four things in your head when there is room
for two. Losing the thread after an interruption and not finding it again.

ADHD, brain injury, long COVID, depression, chronic pain, sleep deprivation,
grief, and the ordinary Thursday afternoon — the mechanism differs, the
experience of a task not starting is similar.

This lesson is narrower than "be more productive". It is about the specific
moment where things stall.

## The stall is a decision, not the work

Here is the thing worth knowing.

When a task will not start, the obstacle is usually not the task. It is that
the first step is undefined, and defining it requires exactly the capacity that
is currently unavailable.

"Sort out the insurance" is not a task. It is a category. Somewhere inside it
is a five-minute action, and finding that action is the hard part.

So hand over the *deciding*, not the doing:

> *I need to [thing]. I have not started. Give me the single smallest first
> step — something I could do in five minutes, right now, that needs no
> decisions from me.*
>
> *Do not give me a plan. One step.*

*Do not give me a plan* matters. A twelve-item plan is a new thing to feel
behind on. You asked for a door, not a map.

## Make the step embarrassingly small

If the returned step still feels unstartable, it is too big. Ask again:

> *Smaller. What is the first thirty seconds?*

"Open the insurance email and read the subject line" is a real step. It sounds
trivial and it works, because the barrier was never the effort — it was the
undefined-ness.

This is not a trick to fool yourself. It is removing the decision that was
blocking a thing you already wanted to do.

## Write down where you were

For interruptions, the useful habit is a **breadcrumb**: before stopping, one
line about where you are and what is next.

Asking an assistant to produce it works well because you can do it out loud
when typing is one thing too many:

> *I am stopping here. Write me three lines: what I was doing, what I had
> decided, and the next action.*

Reading three lines is a far cheaper re-entry than reconstructing the state of a
task from what is on the screen.

## External memory, and its one condition

Offloading to a system rather than holding things in your head is the standard
advice and it is sound. There is one condition attached that people skip.

**Capture must be reliable before you can stop remembering.** If things
sometimes get written down, your head keeps holding everything anyway — and now
you are maintaining two systems and trusting neither.

So make capture take one action. A single note, a single list, one place. An
assistant helps with sorting later; it does not help if the thing never got
captured.

Then, separately:

> *Here is everything I dumped this week. Group it, flag anything with a
> deadline, and tell me what looks urgent.*

Sorting is genuinely a good use. Capturing has to be yours.

## What this will not do

Being straight about it, because overpromising here is its own harm.

This does not treat ADHD or recover a brain injury. It does not replace
medication, therapy, occupational therapy, or an assessment. It does not make
a bad job survivable or a fair workload out of an unfair one.

What it does is remove a specific, repeated friction — deciding the next step —
that costs more than it looks like it should. That is genuinely valuable and it
is not a cure, and anyone selling it as one is selling something.

If a difficulty is persistent and affecting your life, the useful step is an
assessment, not a better prompt.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Pick the thing that has been on your list longest.

Ask for the single smallest first step, forbidding a plan. If it does not feel
startable, ask for something smaller. Then do that step — it should take less
time than reading this paragraph did.

## Recap

The stall is usually the undefined first step, not the work — so hand over the
deciding and forbid a plan, and make the step small enough to feel trivial.
Leave a three-line breadcrumb before interruptions. Offload to a system only
once capture is genuinely reliable. And be clear about the limit: this removes
a repeated friction, it does not treat anything.
