Automatic captioning crossed a threshold in the last few years. For deaf and
hard-of-hearing people, for anyone in a noisy place, and for anyone following a
language they read better than they hear, it went from a novelty to something
you can plan around.

It still fails, and it fails in a pattern worth knowing.

## Where captions break

**Names.** Personal names, company names, product names. They are exactly what
a meeting is about and exactly what a general model has least reason to
recognise.

**Technical vocabulary.** Your industry's terms, acronyms and jargon.

**Crosstalk.** Two people at once produces confident nonsense rather than a
gap.

**Accents.** Accuracy varies substantially by accent, and the variation tracks
how much of that accent was in the training data. This is not a small effect
and it lands hardest on people already less well served.

**Poor audio.** A laptop microphone across a room, a bad connection, an echoing
hall.

Notice how many are properties of *the room*, not the software. That is where
the leverage is.

## Arrange the room, not just the tool

The single most effective change is **one person speaking at a time, close to a
decent microphone**. Everything else is secondary.

Practical things that work, and are reasonable to ask for:

**Say names at the start.** A round of introductions gives you a written list
to correct the transcript against later.

**Ask people to use headsets.** A £20 headset outperforms any amount of
processing.

**Ask for an agenda and materials in advance.** Knowing the topic makes garbled
captions recoverable — you can reconstruct "the Q3 forecast" from a mangled
line if you knew it was coming.

**Ask for a pause between speakers.** Half a second removes most crosstalk
errors.

None of this is a special favour. It is the same request as "please share your
screen", and it makes meetings better for everyone — which is worth saying when
you ask.

## Transcript as well as captions

Live captions scroll away. Ask for a transcript too.

A transcript can be re-read at your pace, searched, corrected, and turned into
actions with E4·L5's structure — including its UNCLEAR section, which does
double duty here: the things the transcript garbled land in it rather than
being smoothed into confident wrong minutes.

Two cautions:

**Consent first.** E4·L5's rule. Recording or transcribing needs everyone to
know and agree, and it is a legal requirement in many places.

**Correct the names before circulating.** A transcript with everybody's name
misspelled is worse than no transcript, and fixing them takes a minute.

## What captions do not give you

Honest limits, because expecting these and not getting them is worse than
knowing.

**Tone.** Whether that was a joke, or annoyance, or hesitation.

**Who is not speaking.** Silence in a transcript looks the same whether the
room agreed or went cold.

**Overlap and interruption.** The texture of a conversation flattens into
sequential lines.

Professional captioners and sign language interpreters convey these, which is
one reason automatic captions are an addition to that provision rather than a
replacement for it. Where a meeting genuinely matters, ask for the human
service — and note that in many jurisdictions employers and public bodies have
a duty to provide it.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Turn on captions in your next call, and afterwards read the transcript.

Mark every place it got a name wrong. That list is the fastest possible measure
of how much to trust it — and if you paste the list of correct names into the
transcript request next time, many tools do noticeably better.

## Recap

Captions became reliable enough to plan around and still fail on names, jargon,
crosstalk, accents and bad audio. Fix the room first — one speaker, a real
microphone, names read out, materials in advance. Take a transcript as well as
captions, get consent, correct the names before circulating, and remember that
tone, silence and interruption do not survive the transfer at all.
