Most of this curriculum has been about your own work. This lesson is about the
moment your choices land on someone else, which happens sooner than people
expect and usually without a decision being made.

## The quiet version

Nobody announces a restructure. It goes like this.

You automate a report you used to ask a colleague for. Then the summaries
someone else prepared. Then the first draft that used to come from the junior
member of the team.

Each step is sensible on its own. Nobody decided anything. And a role that
consisted of those tasks has been hollowed out by a series of individually
reasonable choices, one of which was yours.

That is worth noticing while it is still a series of choices.

## Drudgery is not the same as a role

The strongest case for automation is removing work nobody wants: retyping,
reformatting, copying between systems. Genuinely good, and often the thing
people are most relieved to lose.

But be careful about what surrounds it. A task that looks like drudgery from
outside frequently carries things that are not:

**Judgement.** The person "just formatting the report" is often catching errors
in it.

**Relationships.** The person who chases the numbers knows who to ask and what
their answer means.

**Visibility.** Junior roles are partly a way of being seen. Remove the visible
output and the person becomes invisible, which affects them long before it
affects the work.

**Learning.** That is how the next senior person is made. Automate the whole
apprenticeship and the pipeline stops, quietly, several years before anyone
notices.

## Ask the person

The practical rule, and it is not complicated:

> **Whose work does this change, and have they been asked?**

Not told afterwards. Asked, while it is still a proposal.

Sometimes they will be delighted — it was the worst part of their week.
Sometimes they will point out what the task was actually doing, which you did
not know. Both are outcomes you want before the change, not after.

This is not a veto. It is the difference between a decision made with people and
one made about them, and that difference is remembered.

## Do not hide it

Automating part of your own job and not saying so is tempting, and it creates
two specific problems.

**The expectation is now permanent.** Output went up. Nobody knows why, so the
new level becomes the baseline — including on the day the tool changes, breaks,
or costs money.

**The knowledge does not exist.** When it fails, the person holding it is you,
and you may never have learned the task properly. E4·L8's third category,
arriving with a deadline.

## If the decision is not yours

Often it will not be. You may be the person whose work is being automated, or
the one asked to implement it.

Two things remain available. **Ask what happens to the people** — a question
that has to be answered aloud, and is frequently one nobody has asked. And
**name what the task carried** — the judgement, the checking, the relationships
that were never in the job description and will be missed when they are gone.

Neither guarantees an outcome. Both put information into a decision that was
going to be made without it.

## Try it now (5 minutes)

Think of one thing you automated in the last few months that touched someone
else's work.

Did you ask them? If not, ask now — not for permission, but to find out what
that task was carrying that you could not see.

## Recap

Roles get hollowed out by a sequence of individually reasonable choices, so
notice while it is still a sequence. Ask whose work a change affects before
making it, and look for the judgement, relationships, visibility and learning
that a "drudgery" task may be carrying. Do not hide your own automation — the
expectation outlives the tool.
