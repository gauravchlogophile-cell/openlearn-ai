You have an outline you believe in. Now the prose — and the goal is a draft
that reads as though a specific person wrote it, because a specific person is
going to put their name on it.

## One section at a time

Resist asking for the whole document. Three reasons, and the third is the one
people miss.

A long output is long to read, so you skim, and skimming is how unchecked
claims get through. Sections keep each piece short enough to actually read.

Corrections stay local. Change something in section two of a full draft and
you are re-reading the whole thing to see what else moved.

And a gap in your argument gets written past. Given the whole outline at once,
an assistant will bridge a weak transition with confident connective prose —
*Building on this, it follows that…* — and the weakness disappears under
fluency instead of surfacing. Section by section, the gap is still a gap.

## Give it your writing

E4·L2 covered this for email; long-form makes it more important, because there
is more surface area for a generic voice to show.

Paste 300–500 words you actually wrote. Not your best writing — your *typical*
writing, ideally on a related subject.

> *Below is 400 words I wrote. Match its sentence length, its level of
> formality, and how much it hedges.*
>
> *Now draft section 2 of the outline: [claim]. About 300 words.*
> *Do not open with a topic sentence restating the heading.*
> *Do not end with a summary of what you just said.*

Those two constraints remove the most recognisable structural habits of
undirected drafts.

## Learn your assistant's tells

Every assistant has verbal habits. Once you can see them, you can forbid them
by name, which works far better than asking for "natural" writing.

Common ones: things arriving in threes; *not only… but also*; conclusions that
hedge in both directions so as to commit to nothing; a closing paragraph that
restates the opening; the word *delve*; *it's worth noting that*.

Keep a short list for whatever you use, and paste it in. Naming the specific
habit is a concrete instruction, where "sound human" is an adjective the model
has to interpret.

## The draft is a draft

Everything drafted this way still needs the pass in L3, and the numbers still
need L5. What you have after this lesson is a document with your structure and
approximately your voice — which is a real saving, and is not the same as
something ready to send.

## Try it now (7 minutes)

Take your outline from L1. Paste 400 words of your own writing, then ask for
one section only, with both structural constraints.

Read it aloud. The sentences you would not have said out loud are the tells —
add them to your list.

## Recap

Draft one section at a time so gaps stay visible, supply 300–500 words of your
own typical writing rather than describing a tone, and forbid your assistant's
specific verbal habits by name. What comes out is a draft with your structure
and roughly your voice — not a finished document.
