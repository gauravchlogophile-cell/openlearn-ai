You have a structure you argued for, sections drafted in your voice, criticism
taken, numbers recomputed. What remains is the part that separates a document
that reads as competent from one that does not — and it is a different skill
from writing.

## Section drafting has a specific cost

L2 recommended drafting section by section, and it was the right advice. It has
one predictable consequence: the sections do not quite agree.

The same thing gets three names across four pages. Two sections make the same
point in different words. A figure in the summary contradicts the same figure
in the appendix. A heading survives from an outline you later reorganised and
now describes something else.

None of these are writing errors. They are *assembly* errors, and they are
exactly what a machine is good at finding.

## The consistency pass

> *This is a complete document assembled from separately drafted sections.
> Do not rewrite it. Report only:*
>
> *1. Terms used inconsistently — list each variant and where it appears.*
> *2. Points made more than once, with locations.*
> *3. Any number, date or name that appears twice with different values.*
> *4. Headings that do not match the content beneath them.*
> *5. Claims in the summary that no section supports.*

*Do not rewrite it* keeps this a report you can act on rather than a new draft
you have to re-read from the top.

Item five earns its place. Summaries get written early, the document changes
underneath them, and the summary keeps asserting the original plan. It is the
part most likely to be read and the part most likely to be stale.

## What it cannot check

Then the pass that stays yours, and there are only three questions.

**Is it true?** Not internally consistent — true. A document can be perfectly
coherent and wrong throughout, and consistency checking will report nothing.

**Is it fair to the people in it?** Anyone named, described or implied. Would
they recognise themselves? Would you say it with them present? This one has no
mechanical version at all.

**Will I sign this?** Your name goes on it. Everything in it becomes something
you said, and E4·L2's rule scales: the larger the document, the more of it you
are quietly agreeing to.

## Read it once on paper

An unfashionable suggestion that keeps earning its place: print it, or at
minimum read it in a different format from the one you wrote it in.

You stop seeing the document you have been editing and start seeing the one
that exists. Most people catch something in the first two pages, every time.

## Try it now (7 minutes)

Take a document assembled from parts. Run the five-point consistency report.

Then read the summary against the sections. If the summary promises something
no section delivers, you have found the most valuable defect in the document.

## Recap

Run a consistency report over the assembled document — terms, repetition,
conflicting figures, stale headings, and summary claims no section supports —
and forbid rewriting so the output stays actionable. Then ask the three
questions no tool can: is it true, is it fair to the people in it, and will you
sign it. That completes E5.
