Ask for "a presentation about the Q3 results" and you will get eight slides
with four bullets each, all grammatically parallel, none of which say anything.
It is the most reliably disappointing output in this module.

The problem is not the tool. It is that "about X" describes a topic, and a
presentation is not a topic. It is an argument delivered in order.

## Get the argument first

Before any slides:

> *I need to present [subject] to [audience] in [minutes] minutes.*
>
> *What I want them to do afterwards: [decision].*
>
> *Do not make slides yet. Give me the argument as a numbered sequence of
> claims, where each one is needed for the next. Mark any claim I have not
> given you evidence for.*

That last instruction is doing the same job as L1's sceptical-reader line, and
it is where decks usually fail — a step in the reasoning that everyone assumes
and nobody has shown.

Naming what you want the audience to *do* also disciplines everything
downstream. A deck aimed at a decision is a different deck from one aimed at
"sharing an update", and most bad decks are the second pretending to be the
first.

## One assertion per slide

Now convert. The rule that does the most work:

> *One slide per claim. The title must be the claim, as a full sentence — not
> a topic label. The body carries the single piece of evidence for it. If a
> slide needs two pieces of evidence, split it.*

Compare:

> *Regional performance* — a folder name.
>
> *North is the only region below plan, and it is 12% below* — a claim,
> arguable and checkable.

Assertion headlines have a useful side effect: a reader who only skims your
titles still receives the whole argument. Given how decks actually get consumed
— forwarded, half-read, skimmed on a phone — that is where most of the value
lands.

## What to do with the bullets

Ask for prose, then cut. Bullets are the default because they look like slide
content, but a bullet is a sentence with its verb removed and its logic hidden.

If a slide genuinely holds a list, make it a list of *things* — regions,
options, dates. If the items are ideas, they are separate slides or they are
speaker notes.

Speaker notes are the right home for most of what ends up on slides. Ask for
them explicitly: *give me the two sentences I should say over this slide.*

## The numbers rule still applies

Every figure on a slide is subject to L5. Slides are the format where an
unchecked number does most damage, because it is projected, screenshotted and
quoted long after the deck's context is gone. Recompute anything you put in
30-point type.

## Try it now (7 minutes)

Take a deck you need to build. Ask for the argument as numbered claims, with
unevidenced steps marked.

Then write assertion headlines for the first three. If you cannot state a slide
as a claim, you have found a slide that does not need to exist.

## Recap

Get the argument as numbered claims before any slide exists, mark the steps you
have not evidenced, and give every slide an assertion headline with a single
piece of evidence under it. Push the rest into speaker notes, and recompute
every number you put on screen.
