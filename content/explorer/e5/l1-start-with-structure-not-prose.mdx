Ask for a report and you will get one. Complete, confident, well-organised,
and almost impossible to fix — because by the time you are reading finished
prose, the decisions that mattered have already been made without you.

## Fluent text is hard to argue with

There is a specific trap here worth naming, because careful people fall into
it and do not notice.

When text reads well, you edit at the level of wording. You tighten a
sentence, swap a word, cut a paragraph that repeats. What you do not do is
ask whether the third section should exist at all, because the third section
is *written*, and written things have a weight that outlines do not.

That is sunk cost operating on something that cost you nothing. You did not
write it. It still feels expensive to throw away.

## Ask for the skeleton

So stop earlier. Ask for the structure and nothing else:

> *I need to write [document] for [audience]. Give me an outline only — no
> prose.*
>
> *For each section: the heading, and one sentence stating the claim that
> section makes. Not what it is "about" — what it asserts.*
>
> *Then list what a sceptical reader would need to see before believing it.*

The "claim, not topic" instruction is doing the work. A heading like
*Background* tells you nothing. *Background: the current process was designed
for a team half this size* is a claim — and you can immediately tell whether
you believe it, whether you can evidence it, and whether it belongs.

## Argue with the outline

Now the cheap part. Delete sections. Reorder them. Notice that two make the
same point, or that the one you care about is fourth when it should be first.

This is where the document is actually written. Fifteen minutes here saves an
hour of rewriting fluent prose that was arguing for the wrong shape.

When the outline is right, ask for prose one section at a time — not the whole
thing. Section by section keeps each piece short enough to actually read, and
stops the assistant smoothing over a gap in your argument by writing
confidently past it.

## Where the outline comes from matters

One caution. If you ask for an outline on a subject you know nothing about,
you get a plausible-shaped outline, which is not the same as a good one. It
will reflect how such documents are usually organised, not what your
particular situation needs.

Use it as a prompt for your own thinking — *have I considered this section?* —
rather than as an answer. E4·L1's rule again: this works best where you can
check it, and you can check an outline in your own field at a glance.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Take a document you need to write. Ask for an outline with one claim per
section, plus what a sceptic would need.

Then cut a section. There is almost always one that exists because documents
like this usually have one.

## Recap

Ask for structure before prose, insist each section states a claim rather than
a topic, and do your arguing at the outline stage where changes are cheap.
Then write section by section. Fluent early drafts are not a head start — they
are a decision made without you.
