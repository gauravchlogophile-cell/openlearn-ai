Of all embedded AI, the features that read your correspondence deserve the most
attention. They operate on everything, continuously, and what they touch is
frequently sensitive and rarely only yours.

## Summaries of threads

The "here is what this thread says" box at the top of a long email chain.
Genuinely useful — and it drops exactly the things E4·L3 warned about.

**Conditions.** *"We can do Thursday if the figures arrive by Tuesday"* becomes
*"They agreed to Thursday."*

**Who said what.** In a thread with several people, attribution is where these
most often blur — and in a disagreement it is the only thing that matters.

**The actual ask.** Buried in paragraph four of a long message, which is exactly
where summarisation deprioritises it.

**Tone.** Somebody being carefully polite about being extremely unhappy reads as
neutral.

Use the summary as a map, as E4·L3 taught — to decide which messages to read
properly. Never as the basis for a reply, and never for anything with a
commitment in it.

## Suggested replies

The one-tap responses. Convenient, and they carry a specific risk that is about
you rather than the technology.

They are optimised for **plausibility and speed**. That combination encourages
replying without reading properly — you glance, the suggestion looks right, you
tap.

The failure is not that the reply is wrong English. It is that you have agreed
to something. *"Sounds good!"* on a message whose second paragraph committed you
to a date, a cost, or a position.

**Rule: never use a suggested reply on a message you have not read to the end.**
That sounds obvious and it is precisely the behaviour the feature is designed to
make unnecessary.

For anything with a decision, a number or a person's feelings in it, write your
own — E4·L2's rule about bad news, apologies and disagreement applies at least
as strongly here.

## Priority sorting

Deciding which messages you see first, and which land in a low-priority pile.

The failure mode is invisible by construction: **you cannot notice the message
you were not shown.** Unlike a bad summary, there is no output to inspect.

Two habits worth keeping: look through the deprioritised folder occasionally,
and be aware that anything unusual — an unfamiliar sender, an atypical format,
a first approach from someone who matters — is exactly what a
sorted-by-typicality system handles worst.

## The privacy that is not yours

The part most people never consider, and the most important in this lesson.

Your correspondents wrote to **you**. They did not choose your email provider,
your assistant, or the summarisation feature you enabled. They have no way to
know their message is being processed and no way to object.

That matters most when:

- Someone wrote something **confidential** on the understanding it was going to
  a person.
- The message contains **third-party personal data** — a colleague's health, a
  client's details, someone's address.
- You are in a role where correspondence is **privileged or protected** — legal,
  medical, journalistic, HR, safeguarding, pastoral.

For those roles specifically, an always-on summariser reading everything is a
professional question rather than a preference, and your organisation's rules
almost certainly have something to say about it.

E4·L8's paste test framed this as a decision about what you paste. Embedded
features remove the pasting step — the content goes automatically — which makes
it a settings decision made once, on behalf of everyone who writes to you.

## Try it now (5 minutes)

Find a thread summary in your email. Read the whole thread, then re-read the
summary.

Note what it dropped — most people find a condition or an attribution. Then
decide, deliberately, whether you want that feature on given who writes to you.

## Recap

Treat thread summaries as maps, never as the basis for a reply, because
conditions, attribution and the ask are what they drop. Never send a suggested
reply on a message you have not read to the end. Remember that priority sorting
fails invisibly, since you cannot notice what you were not shown. And recognise
that these features process other people's messages — a decision made once, on
behalf of everyone who writes to you.
