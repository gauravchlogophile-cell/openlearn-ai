Everything in this curriculum has quietly pictured a keyboard and a screen. For
most people on earth the phone is not a secondary device — it is the only one.

This lesson is what changes when that is your situation.

## Voice is the better input

On a phone, dictation usually beats typing for anything longer than a sentence,
and people under-use it because it feels informal.

It is faster. It suits the long, context-rich prompts that E3 showed produce
better answers — you will happily say four sentences of background you would
never thumb-type. And **rambling is fine**: false starts, corrections mid-flow,
"actually, no, what I mean is". Models handle disfluent speech well, and E9·L3's
dictate-then-tidy pattern applies to everyone, not only people who find writing
hard.

Two practical notes: check whether dictation runs on-device or in the cloud
(L2's question), and remember that you are speaking aloud — in an office, a
train, or a shared home, that is a privacy decision about the people around you
as much as the software.

## The camera is an input

The phone's real advantage over a laptop. Point it at:

- A document, a form, a letter — text extraction and explanation.
- A whiteboard after a meeting.
- A product label, an ingredients list, a warning notice.
- A screen showing an error message.
- Something you cannot name and want identified.

E9·L4 covered this as an access tool with its failure modes; the same cautions
hold for everyone. Digits are where it errs, and it will answer confidently
about a blurred image unless you tell it to say when it cannot tell.

For anything with small print, **ask for the exact text first, then the
explanation.** Checkable, quotable, and it exposes a misread before you act on
it.

## On-device changes the calculation

Modern phones run small models locally. Vendors advertise this, because it is a
genuine advantage rather than marketing.

**Works offline.** No signal, no problem — on a train, in a basement, in a
place with patchy coverage.

**Costs no mobile data.** Which matters enormously where data is metered and
expensive.

**Nothing is transmitted**, so E4·L8's paste test resolves differently. Content
that could not go to a cloud service may be fine on-device.

**Less capable**, and adequate for a surprising amount: prediction,
transcription, summarising, tidying.

Where a feature offers a choice, on-device is frequently the better default for
ordinary work, with cloud reserved for things that genuinely need capability.

## The costs nobody mentions

**Mobile data.** Cloud AI features move real amounts of data, and image and
voice features move more. On a metered plan that is money — E11's subject,
arriving in a form its lessons did not cover.

**Battery.** On-device inference is computationally heavy. Sustained use warms
the phone and drains it noticeably.

**Storage.** Downloaded models take gigabytes, on devices where storage is often
the binding constraint.

None of these appears in the feature description. All three are real for someone
using a mid-range phone on a limited plan, which is a large share of the world.

## Practical settings worth checking

Three specific ones on any phone:

**Which features work offline** — so you know what still functions with no
signal.

**Whether AI features are restricted on mobile data** — many can be limited to
Wi-Fi, which is the single most useful setting for a metered plan.

**What the assistant can access** when the phone is locked, and what a voice
assistant does with recordings — L3's territory, and phones are where the
defaults are most permissive.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Dictate a prompt instead of typing it — at least three sentences of context.
Compare the answer to your usual shorter typed version.

Then find your phone's setting for restricting AI features to Wi-Fi. Whether or
not you enable it, knowing it exists is worth the minute.

## Recap

On a phone, dictate rather than type — it is faster and you will give more
context. Use the camera, asking for exact text before explanation. Prefer
on-device where offered: offline, no data cost, nothing transmitted, and
adequate for most ordinary work. And count the mobile-specific costs — data,
battery and storage — which no feature description mentions.
