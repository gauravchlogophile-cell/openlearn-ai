Workplace AI is the version of this module you have least control over. You did
not choose the tools, you frequently cannot switch them off, and the data
involved is not yours to decide about.

What you can do is know what is running and ask good questions.

## Find out what is actually deployed

Most people work alongside AI tooling they could not name. Worth establishing:

**Meeting tools** — recording, transcription, summaries, and whether they run
automatically.

**Communication tools** — summarisation, sentiment analysis, search across
messages.

**Productivity and performance tooling** — activity metrics, "engagement"
scores, code or output analysis.

**Security tooling** — content scanning, data-loss prevention, anomaly
detection. Usually legitimate and usually broader than people assume.

**Recruitment** — screening for roles you might be hiring for. E8·L4's
accountability question lands directly here.

Ask IT, or your manager, or read the intranet. The answer is usually available
and usually unread.

## Policy or rumour?

The most common workplace situation is a confident belief with no document
behind it — *"apparently they can read everything"*, or equally *"nobody's
looking"*. Both are guesses.

Ask for the **written policy**: what tools are in use, what they process, how
long it is retained, who can see it, and what it may be used for. Most
organisations of any size have one, often for compliance reasons, and often
nobody has read it since induction.

If there is no policy, that is itself the finding, and worth saying to somebody
who can commission one.

## Meetings and consent

E4·L5 established that recording needs everyone to know and agree, and that it
is a legal requirement in many places. Inside an organisation this does not
dissolve — colleagues are people, and automatic transcription in a conferencing
tool is still recording.

Practical position: **say at the start that it is on.** If a tool records by
default, the announcement is not optional politeness, it is the thing that makes
it lawful in many jurisdictions.

If someone objects, that is information rather than obstruction. There are
conversations people will not have on the record, and some of them are the ones
you most need to have.

## If you are being measured by it

Where AI-derived metrics feed performance assessment, E8·L4's questions are
yours to ask:

**What exactly is measured, and does it capture the actual job?** Activity
metrics reliably miss thinking, mentoring, and the work of preventing problems
that then do not happen.

**Who reviews it, and can they overrule it?** Nominal oversight — a reviewer
without time, information or authority — is worse than none, because it
manufactures an accountable human who was never in control.

**Can I see my own data?** In many regimes you can request it, and reviewing it
is how errors get found.

## Raising it well

Same shape as E8·L7. A specific question about process gets an answer; a general
objection gets defensiveness.

> *Does the meeting tool record automatically, and where are the transcripts
> stored?*

> *We are summarising customer emails — has that been checked against our data
> protection obligations?*

> *Is the screening tool's output reviewed by a person before rejection?*

In writing, to whoever owns the tool. Notice these are not accusations. Very
often nobody asked, the answer is unsatisfying, and asking is what starts the
work of fixing it.

## Try it now (5 minutes)

Find out one thing: does your organisation have a written policy on AI tools,
and have you read it?

If yes, read the retention and access sections. If no, you have found something
worth raising with somebody who can act on it.

## Recap

Find out what is deployed and get the written policy rather than the rumour —
it usually exists and is usually unread. Announce automatic recording, because
colleagues did not choose it either and consent rules do not stop at the office
door. Where metrics assess you, ask what is measured and whether a person can
overrule it. Raise concerns as specific written questions about process, to
whoever owns the tool.
