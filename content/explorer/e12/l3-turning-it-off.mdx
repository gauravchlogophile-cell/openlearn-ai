Having spotted a feature, you may want it gone — or you may want it and want to
know what it does with your material. Either way, the switches.

## Where they hide

There is no standard. In practice, look in this order:

**The feature's own menu.** The three dots beside the suggestion, or a small
settings icon on the panel itself. Fastest when it exists.

**Account or profile settings**, usually under Privacy, Data, or a section
named after the feature family — "Smart features", "Intelligence", "Assistant".

**A separate privacy dashboard.** Several large providers keep data controls
somewhere entirely different from ordinary settings.

**Per-document or per-conversation**, for some tools.

**Nowhere.** This is a real answer, and the rest of the lesson deals with it.

## Hiding is not disabling

The distinction that matters most, and the one interfaces are vaguest about.

**Hiding** removes the suggestion from your screen. The processing may continue
— the content is still read, still sent, still analysed. You have changed your
view, not the data flow.

**Disabling** stops the processing.

The wording is your only clue, and it is a weak one. *"Show suggestions"* is
almost certainly a display toggle. *"Allow [product] to analyse your messages"*
is closer to the real thing.

When it matters, verify from the privacy documentation rather than the toggle
label. And where only a display toggle exists, treat the processing as ongoing.

## The training opt-out is a separate switch

Frequently missed, because people reasonably assume one control covers it.

Whether your content is used to **train future models** is usually a different
setting from whether a feature appears. You can switch off suggestions and still
be contributing text.

Look for wording about *improving our services*, *model training*, *human
review*, or *quality review*. That last one matters: some services allow staff
to read samples of user content, and it is a distinct question from automated
training.

Business and enterprise tiers frequently exclude training by default, which is
part of what the price buys — E2·L9's territory, and worth confirming rather
than assuming for your specific plan.

## When there is genuinely no off

Increasingly common, and worth being straight about: some features cannot be
disabled. AI summaries in search. Prediction baked into a keyboard. Vendor
processing described as integral to the service.

Your options are narrower and still real:

**Use a different tool for that content.** The sensitive draft goes in a
different editor; the private search goes to a search engine that does not
generate answers.

**Use a local-only option.** E11·L5's argument, arriving as a privacy answer
rather than a cost one.

**Change what you put in.** Names removed, figures altered, the shape of the
problem rather than the thing itself.

**Ask your organisation.** If work data is involved, this is not your decision
alone, and someone is accountable for it.

Choosing not to use a tool for certain content is a legitimate control, and
sometimes the only one you have.

## Do the sweep once

Setting these individually as you meet them means you never finish. Do one pass:

Phone keyboard. Phone assistant and photo features. Email. Documents. Messaging.
Browser. Work tools. Account-level privacy dashboards for each major provider you
use.

An hour, once, then a quick check when something is redesigned — which is when
settings quietly return to their defaults.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Pick the tool holding your most sensitive material. Find its AI settings.

Determine two things: whether the toggle hides or disables, and whether a
separate training opt-out exists. Most people discover the second one exists and
was on.

## Recap

Look in the feature's own menu, then account privacy settings, then a separate
privacy dashboard. Distinguish hiding from disabling, because the wording is
vague and only one stops the processing. Check separately whether your content
trains future models — that is usually its own switch and often on. Where there
is no off, changing what you put in or which tool you use is a legitimate
control. Sweep once, then re-check after redesigns.
