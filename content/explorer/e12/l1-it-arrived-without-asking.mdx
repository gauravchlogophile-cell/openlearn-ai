This whole curriculum has assumed you opened an assistant on purpose. For most
people that is not how it started.

It started with a suggested reply appearing under an email. A summary at the top
of a search page. A phone keyboard finishing sentences. A "write with AI" button
in a document. Nobody chose any of it — it turned up in software they already
had, usually switched on.

## Chosen and unchosen are different situations

When you open an assistant, you know it is one. You bring expectations. You know
to check things, because eleven modules told you to.

Embedded AI removes all three conditions:

**You may not know it is AI.** A "suggested" reply, a "smart" summary, a search
result that reads like an answer. The word AI often appears nowhere.

**You never evaluated it.** E2 taught a fair comparison before choosing. Nobody
ran that on the summariser their email provider enabled last Tuesday.

**It acts by default.** Not when you ask. Continuously, on everything, unless
you find the setting.

That combination is why this module exists. Everything you have learned still
applies — it just has to be applied to something you did not go looking for.

## Where it already is

Not exhaustive, but enough to recognise the pattern:

**Email** — suggested replies, tone rewriting, thread summaries, priority
sorting.

**Documents and spreadsheets** — drafting, rewriting, formula generation,
summarising.

**Search** — generated answers above the results. For many people this is now
their main AI use and they have never called it that.

**Phones** — keyboard prediction, photo editing and search, voice assistants,
call transcription, on-device summaries.

**Messaging** — suggested replies, translation, chat summaries.

**Customer service** — the first responder is frequently a model.

**Work software** — meeting notes, ticket routing, CV screening, performance
tooling.

## The default is the decision

A quiet but important point.

Most people never change a default setting. So whoever picks the default is
effectively deciding the behaviour for almost everyone — and defaults are chosen
by the vendor, in their interest, which is usually "on".

That is not sinister; it is how software has always worked. But it means
**"I did not turn this on" and "I agreed to this" are different statements**,
and only the second one implies you considered it.

L3 is about finding those switches. L2 is about noticing there is something to
switch.

## What does not change

Reassuringly, the substance transfers directly:

- **E7 still applies.** A generated search summary needs the same checking as a
  chatbot answer, and gets less of it because it looks like a search result.
- **E4·L8's paste test still applies.** An email assistant reads your email. A
  meeting summariser hears your meeting.
- **E9's caution still applies.** If a captioning feature is doing the work,
  know its failure modes.

You are not learning a new subject. You are pointing an existing one at
something that did not announce itself.

## Try it now (5 minutes)

Open the app you use most for email or messaging. Look for anything offering to
write, summarise, suggest or organise.

Count them. Most people find between two and five, and are surprised by at least
one — that surprise is the point of this module.

## Recap

Most people's AI arrived as a feature in software they already had, switched on
by default. That removes the three things that make deliberate use safer —
knowing it is AI, having evaluated it, and it acting only when asked. Everything
you have learned still applies; it now has to be applied to something that did
not announce itself.
