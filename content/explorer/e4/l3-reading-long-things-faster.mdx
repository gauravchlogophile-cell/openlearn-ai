Summarising is the most-used feature in this entire module and the one most
likely to quietly mislead you. Both things are true at once, and the difference
comes down to what you do with the summary afterwards.

## What a summary is for

A summary is a **map**. It tells you the shape of a document and where the
interesting parts are, so you can read those properly.

It is not a replacement for having read the thing. The moment you start
repeating a summary's claims as your own knowledge, you are asserting things
you have not checked, in a form that sounds like you checked them.

L1's rule applies exactly here. Summarising a thread you were in is cheap to
verify — you were there. Summarising a report you have never opened is not, and
that is the case where an error survives.

## Ask for structure, not prose

A paragraph summary invites you to accept it and move on. A structured one
gives you somewhere to push back:

> *Summarise the document below as:*
> *1. The claim it is making, in one sentence.*
> *2. The three pieces of evidence it leans on hardest.*
> *3. Anything it explicitly says it is NOT claiming.*
> *4. Any numbers, with their units and dates.*
> *5. Questions a sceptical reader would ask that it does not answer.*

Point three and point five are the valuable ones. Documents are careful about
what they exclude, and a generic summary flattens exactly that care. Asking
what the document declines to claim recovers it.

## Make it quote

Add one instruction and the reliability changes:

> *For each point, include the sentence from the source it came from, in
> quotation marks.*

A quote is **checkable**. You can search the document for it. A paraphrase is
not, and paraphrase is where a hedge quietly disappears — "may reduce costs in
some settings" becomes "reduces costs", and the summary is now wrong in the
direction you would most like it to be right.

If a quote does not appear in the source when you search for it, you have
learned something important about that summary and everything else in it.

## What summaries lose

Even a good summary systematically drops three things:

- **Hedging.** *Preliminary*, *in this sample*, *further work is needed*.
- **Conditions.** The circumstances under which the claim holds.
- **Dissent.** Minority positions, dissenting notes, the paragraph that
  disagrees with the rest.

These are dropped because summarisation optimises for the main thrust, and
qualifiers are not the main thrust. But in a report, a contract or a paper,
those qualifiers are frequently the entire point.

So when the stakes are real, read the original sections your map pointed at.
The summary got you there in a tenth of the time. That was the win.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Take a long document you actually need to read. Ask for the five-point
structure above, with quotes.

Then search the document for two of the quotes. Confirm they exist and that the
surrounding sentence means what the summary implied. This check takes a minute
and will tell you how much to trust the rest.

## Recap

Treat a summary as a map into the document, ask for structure rather than
prose, require quotes so claims stay traceable, and remember that hedging,
conditions and dissent are the first things lost. Where the stakes are real,
the summary tells you what to read — it does not tell you that you have read it.
