Ask for a project plan and you will get one: neat phases, tidy durations,
sensible-looking milestones. It will be genuinely useful in one respect and
quietly misleading in another, and the two are easy to confuse because they
arrive in the same table.

## Good at steps

Decomposition is a real strength. A model has read an enormous number of
descriptions of how work gets done, so it is good at listing the steps
something involves — including the unglamorous ones people forget.

Ask for the plan you have not thought of:

> *I need to [goal] by [date]. Here is what I have already thought of: [list].*
>
> *What steps am I missing? Include the boring ones — approvals, access,
> waiting on other people, things that must happen before step one can start.
> Do not give me timings.*

Two parts do the work. Telling it what you already have stops it repeating your
own list back. And *do not give me timings* keeps the output in the area where
it is reliable.

The answer to "what has to be true before step one can start" is where most
plans actually fail. Access not granted. A form nobody knew existed. A person
on leave.

## Bad at durations

Now the part to distrust.

A duration in a plan looks like a measurement. It is not — it is a plausible
number, drawn from how long tasks like this are *described* as taking, in
general, by people writing on the internet.

It knows nothing about your approval chain, your team's other commitments, how
long your organisation takes to answer an email, or the fortnight in December
when nothing moves. It is also systematically optimistic, because written
accounts of work are optimistic; people describe the intended path far more
often than the one with three weeks of waiting in it.

So take the steps and put your own numbers against them. You know things the
model structurally cannot.

## Ask what would go wrong

The other genuinely strong move is inverting the plan:

> *Assume this plan failed. It is [date] and we missed it. Give the five most
> likely reasons, most likely first. Be specific — "poor communication" is not
> a reason.*

This is a pre-mortem, and it works well because failure modes are heavily
documented. Named specifically, several will be things you can cheaply prevent
now: a dependency nobody owns, an approval with a two-week queue, one person
holding three critical steps.

## Keep the plan yours

A plan is a commitment you will be held to, which puts it firmly in L1's
expensive-to-check column. Use the assistant for the shape — steps,
dependencies, failure modes — and own the judgement: what matters, what can
slip, what you will actually do when it does.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Take something you are planning. List the steps you have thought of, then ask
what is missing, explicitly excluding timings.

Then run the pre-mortem. Pick the one failure reason that made you wince and
deal with it today.

## Recap

Use an assistant to find the steps and dependencies you missed, and to run a
pre-mortem on a plan you already have. Do not take its durations — they are
plausible numbers, not measurements of your situation, and they lean
optimistic. The steps can be borrowed; the commitment stays yours.
