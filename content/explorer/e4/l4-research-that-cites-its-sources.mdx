E2·L6 introduced answer engines — tools that search first and then write, with
citations attached. For research they are a real improvement on an assistant
answering from memory, because there is something to check.

The improvement is that checking became *possible*. It did not become
*automatic*, and the footnotes are persuasive enough that most people stop
there.

## A citation is a receipt, not a proof

When an answer arrives with four superscript numbers, it is easy to read that
as four independent confirmations. It is not. It means four pages were
retrieved and used while composing.

Three things can go wrong, and all three are common:

- **The source does not say it.** The page was retrieved for topical overlap
  and the specific claim was assembled around it.
- **The source is weak.** A press release, a vendor's own marketing, a forum
  post, a summary of a summary.
- **The source is stale.** True in 2023, superseded since — a particular
  problem for anything about model capabilities or pricing.

None of these look different from a correct citation until you open it.

## The one-minute check

Do this for any claim you intend to repeat, act on, or put your name near:

**Does the link open?** A dead or redirecting link is a bad sign on its own.

**Does the page actually say it?** Search the page for the specific number or
phrase. Not the topic — the claim. This is where most failures surface.

**Would you cite this source yourself?** If the answer rests on a vendor's own
blog post, you have a marketing claim with a footnote.

Under a minute for the ones that matter. Skipping it means you have outsourced
not the searching but the judging, which was the part that needed you.

## Ask questions that can fail

How you ask shapes what comes back. A question with an answer baked in gets
that answer confirmed, because agreeable material exists for almost every
position.

> *Why is [approach] better than [alternative]?*

...retrieves the case for it. Instead, ask in a way that permits a no:

> *What is the strongest evidence AGAINST [claim]? Who disagrees, and on what
> grounds? If the evidence is mixed, say so rather than picking a side.*

That last clause matters. Without permission to report uncertainty, a model
will usually resolve it for you, and you will never see that it was there.

## Where this leads

You are doing a light version of something E7 covers properly — that module is
compulsory for certification and goes considerably deeper into verification and
manipulation.

For now, one habit: **the citation tells you where to look, not what is true.**
Same shape as the previous lesson. A summary is a map to a document; a citation
is a map to a source. Neither is the destination.

## Try it now (7 minutes)

Ask an answer engine a factual question in your own field where you already
know the answer — that is the point, since it lets you grade the result.

Open every citation. Check whether each page contains the specific claim. Then
ask the same question in the "strongest evidence against" form and see whether
anything appears that the first answer left out.

## Recap

Answer engines make checking possible rather than unnecessary. Open the links,
search for the specific claim, and judge whether the source is one you would
cite. Ask questions that allow a no, and let the answer say the evidence is
mixed — a citation points at a source, it does not vouch for it.
