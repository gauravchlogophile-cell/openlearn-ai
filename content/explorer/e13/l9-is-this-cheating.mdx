This is the question people actually ask about AI and learning, in far greater
numbers than anything else in this module. It deserves a direct answer rather
than a disclaimer.

## Two questions wearing one coat

"Is this cheating?" is really two questions, and they have different answers.

**Does it break the rules I am under?** A factual question about a specific
institution, course or employer. It has an answer, you can find it out, and
getting it wrong has consequences.

**Does it defeat the point of what I am doing?** A question about you. Nobody
enforces it, and it is the one that determines whether you end up able to do the
thing.

You can break the rules while learning a great deal, and follow every rule while
learning nothing. Most people only think about the first.

## The rules question

**Find out rather than assume.** Institutions differ enormously — some ban it,
some require disclosure, some encourage it, and many now distinguish between
uses. The same university frequently has different rules per course.

**Ask in writing if it is unclear**, before you submit. *"May I use an AI
assistant to check my grammar / generate practice questions / explain a concept
I am stuck on?"* Specific questions get specific answers, and you keep the
reply.

**Assume tighter rules for assessed work.** The gap between "helped me study"
and "helped me produce the thing being marked" is where nearly every policy
draws its line.

**Assistive use is usually treated differently.** E9·L3's point: if you use
dictation or spelling correction because of a disability, that is generally
provided for rather than prohibited — but confirm it with the institution rather
than deciding for yourself.

## The self-defeat question

This one no policy covers, and it is E13's actual subject.

Ask: **what was this task for?**

An essay is not valuable because the world needed another essay. It exists to
make you think through an argument. A problem set exists to build the reactions
in E13·L7. A reading exists to put something in your head.

So the test is not how much you typed. It is:

> **Did the thing the task was designed to produce in me actually happen?**

By that test:

**Not self-defeating** — having a concept explained, generating practice
questions, checking your own work for errors, being told where your argument is
weak, tidying grammar on something you wrote and understand.

**Self-defeating** — having the argument constructed for you, receiving worked
solutions instead of attempting them, submitting a summary of a reading you did
not do.

Notice the shape: **anything that adds a check to your own thinking is safe;
anything that replaces your thinking with output is not.** That is the same line
E8·L1 drew for work, arriving in the place it matters most.

## The honest complication

Two things worth admitting.

**Detection is unreliable.** AI-detection tools produce false positives, and
they do so disproportionately for non-native English writers — people who write
in a plainer, more regular register get flagged for it. If you are accused
wrongly, ask what evidence exists beyond a detector score, and provide drafts,
version history and notes. Keeping those as you work is cheap insurance.

**The rules are unsettled and moving.** What was banned in 2023 is required in
some courses now. That is uncomfortable and it does not excuse ignoring the
rules you are actually under today.

## The question to sit with

If your use came out — to your tutor, your examiner, your employer — would you
be comfortable explaining it?

That is E8·L2's disclosure test, and here it does most of the work. Discomfort
is usually information: not proof you have done wrong, but a reliable signal
that the question deserves a real answer rather than an avoided one.

## Try it now (5 minutes)

Find the actual AI policy for your course, institution or employer. Read it.

Most people have never done this and are operating on a rumour (E12·L7's
finding, in a different setting). If it is unclear, write the specific question
you would send — and send it.

## Recap

Separate the two questions. The rules are factual — find them, ask in writing
where they are unclear, and expect assessed work to be treated more tightly.
Self-defeat is about whether the task's purpose still happened in you: adding a
check to your thinking is safe, replacing your thinking is not. Detection is
unreliable and biased against non-native writers, so keep your drafts. And if
you would not be comfortable explaining your use, that discomfort is worth
listening to.
