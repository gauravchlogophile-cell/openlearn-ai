Seven lessons of technique, and technique is not the reason people fail at this.

They fail because they stop. So this lesson is mostly about not stopping.

## The loop

Four steps, in order, repeating:

**1. Learn a little.** One concept. L2's prompts — anchored to what you know,
analogy with its edge, what beginners misunderstand. Deliberately less than you
could manage, because step two is where the value is and it needs the time.

**2. Attempt it.** L7. Write it, build it, solve it, say it. Badly and
immediately, before it feels ready.

**3. Test yourself later.** L3, the next day rather than the same one. Answer
fully before checking. Ask what you got subtly wrong.

**4. Teach it back.** L5, at the end of the week, on the whole thing rather than
one piece. This is where the structure gets checked.

Underneath all four, **review cards** (L4) tick along daily for the facts worth
retaining.

Notice the proportions: one part explanation, three parts producing something.
Most people's study is the reverse, and L1 explains why it feels better that
way.

## Make it small enough to survive

E4·L7's argument, and it applies harder here because nothing external is holding
you to it.

Set the session at what you could complete on your **worst realistic day** —
tired, busy, unmotivated. Twenty minutes is a real amount. Two hours is a plan
for the version of you that is well-rested and free, and that person does not
turn up in week three.

You can always do more on a good day. You cannot retroactively do a week you
skipped.

## Supply what the assistant will not

L6's honest gap: it has no stake in whether you continue. So build the stake
externally.

**A fixed time.** Same slot, in the calendar. Decisions are what get skipped;
a recurring appointment removes the decision.

**Something that expects you.** A course with deadlines, a study partner, a
class, someone you have told. Anything that notices your absence outperforms
willpower.

**A visible record.** Days done, cards reviewed, things attempted. Lrnon's
streak and review count exist for this — not as a game, but because a visible
record of turning up is what carries you through the fortnight when it stops
being interesting.

**A finish line.** "Learn Python" never ends. "Build this specific thing by
March" does, and a defined end is what makes a beginning meaningful.

## Review it monthly

Three questions, once a month, and the third is the one:

**What can I now do that I could not?** Not what have I covered — what can I
*do*. Covered is not learned.

**What have I been reading about instead of practising?** L7's trap, caught
early.

**Is this still the right thing to be learning?** People continue courses long
after the reason expired, because stopping feels like failing. It is not.

## The honest ending

Most self-directed learning is abandoned, and the reason is almost never that
the material was too hard. It is that nothing was holding it in place.

Everything in this module is designed around that: small sessions that survive
bad weeks, external structure because the tool provides none, production over
consumption because production is what actually teaches, and a review that
permits you to stop.

If you take one thing, take the loop's proportions. One part explanation, three
parts producing something. That single ratio distinguishes people who learn from
people who study.

## Try it now (7 minutes)

Write your loop on one line: what you are learning, when, for how long, and what
expects you.

Put the first session in your calendar now. Then do step one of it today — not
tomorrow, and smaller than you think it should be.

## Recap

Learn a little, attempt it immediately, test yourself the next day, teach it
back at the end of the week, with review cards underneath — one part explanation
to three parts production. Size the session for your worst day, and supply
externally what the tool will not: a fixed time, something that expects you, a
visible record and a finish line. Review monthly on what you can now *do*, and
allow yourself to stop. That completes E13.
