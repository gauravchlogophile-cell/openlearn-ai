You can read every explanation of how to ride a bicycle and fall off. Nobody
finds that surprising.

The same is true of a great many things people do try to learn by reading, and
it is worth naming which.

## Knowing how and being able to

Two different kinds of knowledge.

**Knowing how it works** — the concepts, the principles, the vocabulary. This is
what explanation delivers, and everything up to L6 was about acquiring it
efficiently.

**Being able to do it** — judgement built from repetition. Knowing which
approach fits this case, noticing when something is off, recovering when it goes
wrong.

The second is built by **doing the thing and getting feedback**, repeatedly, and
there is no substitute. It is not stored as facts and cannot be transferred as
explanation.

## Where this catches people

Skills that feel learnable by reading and are not:

**Writing.** You can know every principle in E5 and write badly. Writing is
improved by writing and being read.

**Diagnosis** — medical, mechanical, technical. Pattern recognition built from
having seen many cases, including the ones that looked like something else.

**Conversation** — negotiation, interviewing, teaching, difficult conversations.
E9·L7's rehearsal exists precisely because reading about it does nothing.

**Craft and physical skill.** Cooking, playing an instrument, sport, surgery,
carpentry.

**Design and taste.** Recognising that something is not working yet, which is
built from having made many things that were not working.

**Code.** You can follow every explanation and be unable to build the thing.

Common thread: **the knowledge lives in your reactions**, and reactions are
trained by exposure rather than told.

## The trap this module could create

Everything before this lesson made acquiring explanations more efficient. That
efficiency creates a specific risk.

Reading feels productive. It is comfortable, it is measurable — pages, videos,
lessons — and it produces the fluency L1 warned about. So it is very easy to
spend six months efficiently learning *about* something and never once doing it.

That is worse than inefficient. It builds the confident, articulate
half-knowledge that E8·L1 warned about: you can discuss it and you cannot do it,
and you will not find out until it matters.

## Where an assistant genuinely helps

Around the loop, not inside it:

**Designing practice.** *"I want to get better at X. Give me five exercises,
increasing in difficulty, that I can do this week."*

**Generating cases.** Unlimited practice scenarios, examples to diagnose,
prompts to respond to. This is real value — cases used to be the scarce thing.

**Reviewing an attempt.** E5·L3's critique, applied to work you produced.
Specific objections, no praise.

**Explaining the failure.** After you have tried and it went wrong, an
explanation of why lands in a way the same explanation beforehand would not.
The attempt creates the hook.

**Rehearsal**, per E9·L7, for conversational skills.

What it cannot do is the repetition. Nobody can do that part for you, which is
the whole reason it works.

## The order that works

**Explanation first, briefly.** Enough to attempt it — not everything.

**Attempt it.** Badly. Early.

**Get feedback.** From the assistant, a person, or reality.

**Then explanation again**, targeted at what went wrong.

Most people invert the first two and spend the majority of their time in step
one, where it is comfortable.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Pick a skill you have been reading about rather than doing.

Ask for five exercises, increasing in difficulty. Do the first one now, badly.
Then ask for critique on what you produced.

## Recap

Knowing how something works and being able to do it are different, and the
second is built only by doing and getting feedback. Writing, diagnosis,
conversation, craft, design and code all fall in that category. Use an assistant
around the practice loop — designing it, generating cases, critiquing attempts,
explaining failures — and never in place of the repetition. Explain briefly,
attempt early, then explain again.
