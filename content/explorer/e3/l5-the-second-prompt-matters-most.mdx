## The first answer is diagnostic

You cannot see your own prompt from outside your head. You know what you
meant, so you can't tell what you actually said. The first answer solves this
— it shows you how your request read to something with no access to your
intentions.

If it came back too formal, you didn't specify tone. Too long, you didn't
specify length. About the wrong thing entirely, your task verb was ambiguous
or your context was missing. That is not a wasted round; that's the checklist
from L2 telling you which box was empty.

## Say why, not just what

The instinct is to reject and re-ask: *"no, try again."* That reliably
produces a variation on the thing you just rejected, because nothing told it
what was wrong.

Compare:

- *"Shorter."* → shorter, still wrong in the same way.
- *"Cut it to 150 words. The problem is the opening — it apologises before
  saying what we're doing about it, and that's what made the customer escalate
  last time."*

The second one hands over the reasoning. Now the next attempt can be different
in kind rather than merely in length.

This is the same principle as L3's "what you already rejected", applied in
real time.

## Three follow-ups worth having ready

- **"What did you assume?"** Surfaces the invented specifics before you build
  on them. Frequently the most valuable question in the whole conversation.
- **"Give me three versions that differ in approach, not wording."** Stops you
  converging on your first idea. Genuinely different options, not synonyms.
- **"What's the strongest objection to this?"** Useful precisely because the
  default register is agreeable. You have to ask for the pushback.

## Know when to restart

Iteration has a failure mode. A long thread accumulates every rejected draft,
and all of it is still in the context window shaping what comes next. Past a
certain point you're not steering, you're dragging.

When a conversation has drifted, open a fresh one and write a single clean
prompt containing everything you learned. Faster than a dozen more nudges, and
usually better.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Take any answer you got in an earlier lesson and run three follow-ups: *what
did you assume*, then a why-grounded correction, then *what's the strongest
objection to this*.

Notice that you learned more in three replies than in three fresh prompts.
