There is a persistent belief that skilled prompting means knowing special
words. It mostly means being bothered to paste the thing.

Compare these two requests. *"I've got a client email that's a bit passive
aggressive about a delay, help me reply."* Versus the same sentence with the
actual email underneath it. The first makes the model invent a plausible
email and answer that. The second lets it answer yours.

## Describing is inventing by proxy

When you describe material instead of supplying it, every detail you omit gets
filled in by whatever is statistically typical. You said "a bit passive
aggressive" — the model imagines a passive-aggressive email. It is now
drafting a reply to a message that does not exist.

The reply will read well. It will be subtly wrong about the specifics, and the
specifics are the entire reason you asked.

This is the single biggest gap between people who find these tools useful and
people who find them disappointing, and it has nothing to do with wording.

## What counts as context

More than documents:

- **The source material** — the email, the draft, the spec, the transcript.
- **The constraint** — "under 400 words", "must not promise a refund",
  "our legal team won't approve the word *guarantee*".
- **What you already rejected** — "not another apology-first opening, I've
  tried that and they escalated". This is enormously useful and almost nobody
  supplies it.
- **The goal behind the goal** — "I'm trying to keep this account, not win
  the argument."

That third one is worth dwelling on. Without it, iteration walks in circles:
you reject an answer, ask again, and get a variation on the thing you rejected
because you never said why.

## What to leave out, deliberately

Context is powerful, which is exactly why it needs a boundary. Before you
paste, ask: *would I email this to a stranger?*

- **Other people's personal data.** Your colleague's medical note is not
  yours to paste, whatever the privacy policy says.
- **Credentials and keys.** Never. Not to summarise, not to debug.
- **Anything under confidentiality** you haven't checked the tool against —
  many workplaces have an approved tool and a forbidden one, and the
  difference is contractual, not technical.

Redacting works better than people expect. Replace names with *Client A*,
figures with *£X*, and the structural advice you get back is unaffected.

## When the material is big

For long or numerous sources, source-grounded tools are built for exactly this
job — you load documents and answers stay tied to them, with citations back to
the passage.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Take a real document you need something done with. First, ask your assistant
for help while only *describing* it. Then paste the document and ask again.

Read both. The first answer is about a document that never existed. Notice how
confident it was anyway — that's E1·L7 demonstrated on material where you
happen to know the truth.

## Recap

Supply the material rather than describing it, include your constraints and
your rejected attempts, and draw a firm line at other people's data and
credentials. Next: telling it what "good" looks like before it guesses.
