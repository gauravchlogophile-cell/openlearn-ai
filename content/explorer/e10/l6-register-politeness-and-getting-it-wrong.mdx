In English, politeness is mostly vocabulary and phrasing — you can be casual or
formal, and getting it slightly wrong is a small mistake.

In a great many languages it is **grammar**. The verb form changes, the pronoun
changes, whole registers are marked. Choosing wrong is not a stylistic slip; it
is an insult, or an embarrassing display of deference, and native speakers hear
it immediately.

## Where this applies

Not an exhaustive list, but the shape of the problem:

**T-V distinctions** — *tu/vous*, *tú/usted*, *du/Sie*, *ты/вы*. Two ways to
say "you", and picking the wrong one is a statement about the relationship.

**Honorific systems** — Japanese *keigo* with its distinct humble and respectful
registers; Korean speech levels; Javanese with entirely different vocabulary
sets by register.

**Grammatical respect** — Hindi *आप/तुम/तू*; Tamil, Bengali and most South
Asian languages marking respect in verb forms and pronouns.

**Age and status marking** — many languages where address depends on relative
age, and getting it wrong is a real breach.

## What models do with this

Two failure patterns, both quiet.

**Flattening.** The output picks a middle register and stays there, losing
distinctions the language requires you to make. It reads as translated, because
it is — English-shaped politeness rendered into a language that marks more.

**Defaulting to formal.** Frequently the safer choice and frequently wrong.
Excessive formality with a close friend or a younger sibling is not neutral; it
reads as cold, sarcastic, or as distance being deliberately created.

Neither announces itself. The sentence is grammatical, and the register is
wrong.

## Say the relationship, not the tone

The fix is E3·L3's rule in this specific setting: supply context rather than an
adjective.

Not *"be polite"*, which means nothing across these systems. Instead:

> *Write this in Japanese. I am a junior employee writing to a client's senior
> manager, whom I have not met. Use appropriate keigo.*

> *Write this in Hindi. I am writing to my mother-in-law. Use आप throughout.*

> *Write this in German. A colleague I have worked with for years and address
> as du.*

Who you are, who they are, the relationship, and — where you know it — the
specific form to use. That last clause is worth adding whenever you are sure,
because it removes the guess entirely.

## Back-translation will not save you

L3's warning, stated again because this is exactly where it bites.

English does not mark most of these distinctions. A Japanese sentence in plain
form and the same sentence in keigo both back-translate to the same English.
The check passes and the message is still wrong.

**Register can only be checked by someone who speaks the language.** For
anything sent to a person who matters — a client, an official, a relative, an
in-law — that check is the one worth asking for, and it takes a speaker ten
seconds.

## When you are not sure yourself

Common and worth addressing. People writing in a heritage language, or a
language they learned as an adult, often know the vocabulary and not the social
rules.

Two useful moves:

> *Explain which politeness level you used and why, before the translation.*

You learn the rule rather than just receiving output, which compounds over time.

> *Give me the same message at two politeness levels, and tell me which
> relationships each suits.*

Choosing between two labelled options is far more reliable than judging one, and
it teaches you the distinction.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Write a short message in a language with honorifics or a T-V distinction. Ask
for it twice — once with no relationship specified, once with the full context.

If you can, show both to a speaker and ask which they would send. That answer is
the lesson.

## Recap

Many languages encode respect grammatically, and models either flatten the
distinction or default to formal — both quietly. State who is writing, to whom,
in what relationship, and name the form when you know it. Back-translation is
blind here because English does not mark these, so have a speaker check anything
that matters. When unsure yourself, ask for two labelled levels and learn the
rule.
