Language learning is one of the strongest uses in this whole curriculum, and it
has a specific trap that matters more here than almost anywhere.

## What it is genuinely good at

**Unlimited patient practice.** You can have the same conversation forty times.
You can be slow, wrong and repetitive. No teacher has that patience and no
friend should be asked for it.

**Correction without embarrassment.** For many adult learners, the barrier is
not difficulty but the discomfort of being wrong in front of a person. That
barrier is simply absent here, and it is the reason some people progress with a
machine after stalling for years.

**Explanation on demand.** *Why* is it that form? Ask, at the moment of
confusion, as many times as you need.

**Graded input.** *"Rewrite this at the level of someone six months in."*
Comprehensible material at your level is what actually drives progress, and it
has always been the hardest thing to find.

## The trap

It will teach you things that are wrong, fluently, and you will have no way to
tell — because you are learning the language and cannot evaluate the answer.
That is the definition of the situation.

E7 said verify. E9·L1 said verification assumes you can. Here the assumption
fails by construction: **if you could check it, you would not be learning it**.

## Where it is specifically unreliable

**Colloquial usage.** It knows what is grammatical. What people actually say —
and what sounds strange despite being correct — is much weaker.

**Regional variation.** Which Spanish? Which Arabic? Ask for a specific variety
and it will comply and then drift, mixing forms from elsewhere.

**Naturalness judgements.** *"Would a native speaker say this?"* produces a
confident answer that is frequently wrong. It is a plausibility judgement, not a
report from a speech community.

**Lower-resource languages.** Everything in L1 applies with full force. For
these, an assistant may be worse than no resource, because a confident wrong
teacher is worse than an absent one.

## Practical rules

**Use it for practice, not as your only authority.** Pair it with a textbook, a
course, a dictionary, or a class. Those have been checked by people.

**Ask for the rule, not just the fix.** *"Correct this, and tell me the rule I
broke."* You learn a pattern rather than one sentence, and rules are easier to
verify elsewhere.

**Pin the variety in every session.** *"Mexican Spanish throughout. If a form
differs elsewhere, say so."* Then watch for drift, because it will drift.

**Verify anything you plan to actually say** with a speaker, a dictionary, or a
corpus. Especially idioms and anything that sounded surprising.

**Get real input too.** Native material — video, radio, podcasts, books,
comments sections. A model produces plausible-average language; real speakers
produce the actual thing, including the parts no model foregrounds.

## For the language you half-know

The largest group here, and the one this suits best: people with a heritage
language they understand and cannot write, or one from school that has faded.

You have the ear already. What is missing is production, and production is
exactly what unlimited low-stakes practice supplies. Your existing ear is also
the check the complete beginner lacks — when something sounds off, it probably
is, and that instinct is worth more than the model's confidence.

Ask it to speak to you only in that language, at a level slightly above
comfortable, and correct you as you go. That single arrangement is the best
version of this in the whole lesson.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Start a conversation in a language you are learning. Ask it to stay in that
language, at a level slightly above comfortable, and to correct you with the
rule each time.

Then take one correction and verify it in a dictionary or with a speaker. That
habit is what keeps this useful.

## Recap

Unlimited patient practice, correction without embarrassment and graded input
make this genuinely strong — against a trap you cannot inspect, since being
unable to evaluate the answer is what learning means. Ask for the rule not just
the fix, pin the regional variety and watch for drift, verify anything you plan
to say, and get real native input alongside. Strongest of all for a language you
already half-know.
