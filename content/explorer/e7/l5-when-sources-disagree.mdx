You checked, and now you have two credible sources saying different things.

This feels like failure. It is usually the most informative outcome available —
you have found the exact spot where the question is harder than it looked, and
most people never get there.

## Four reasons, and only one is "someone is wrong"

Before picking a side, work out which situation you are in.

**They measured different things.** "Unemployment" has several definitions.
"Users" might mean accounts, monthly actives, or devices. Two correct numbers,
two different quantities. This is the most common case by a wide margin.

**They measured at different times.** Both were right. One is older. Especially
common with anything about technology, pricing or regulation.

**They used different methods.** Different populations, different sample sizes,
different controls. Both may be sound and describe different slices of reality.

**One is actually wrong.** Real, and considerably rarer than the first three.

Diagnosing which of the four you are in usually resolves the disagreement
without either source having to lose.

## The question that does the work

> **What would have to be true for both to be right?**

Ask it before deciding who to believe. Most of the time there is an answer, and
finding it teaches you more about the subject than picking a winner would.

When there genuinely is no such answer, you have established a real conflict —
and now it is worth spending time on.

## False balance

The opposite failure, and the more damaging one.

Not every disagreement is an even contest. When a large, well-evidenced body of
work is set against a small contrarian position, presenting them as "two sides"
misrepresents the state of knowledge.

Assistants are prone to this. Trained to be balanced and to avoid appearing
partisan, they will often present a lopsided question as a debate, giving each
"side" a comparable paragraph. That is a stylistic default, not a finding about
the evidence.

So ask about the **distribution**, not just the positions:

> *How much support does each position have? Is this an even split among people
> who study it, or a strong consensus with a minority objection? Say which.*

"Some say X, others say Y" is not neutrality. It is a claim about the balance of
evidence, usually made without checking.

## When it is genuinely unsettled

Some questions do not have an answer yet. Long-term effects of recent
technologies. Most contested policy. Anything actively being researched.

The honest move is to say so — and to say what *would* settle it. "This is
unresolved; the studies that would answer it are running now" is a stronger,
more useful position than a manufactured conclusion.

Manufacturing certainty to sound decisive is the actual failure here, and it is
one people commit to avoid seeming unsure. E6·L4 did this deliberately on the
artist-style question, and E7·L8 will ask you to do it in your own work.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Find two sources disagreeing on something in your field. Work out which of the
four reasons applies.

Then ask what would have to be true for both to be right. Note whether the
disagreement survives that question.

## Recap

Diagnose disagreement before resolving it — different things, different times,
different methods, or someone wrong — and ask what would have to be true for
both to hold. Watch for false balance, which assistants produce by default, and
when a question is genuinely open, say so and say what would close it.
