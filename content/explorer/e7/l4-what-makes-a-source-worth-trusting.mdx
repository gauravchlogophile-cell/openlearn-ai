L3 said go to the primary source. This lesson is about judging what you find
when you get there — because "primary" and "reliable" are not the same thing.

The instincts most people use here are close to useless. A polished site costs
very little. A confident tone costs nothing at all. Institutional letterhead
tells you an institution exists.

Four properties do considerably better.

## 1. It shows its evidence

Good sources let you get behind the claim: data, method, the actual document,
a link to the thing itself.

Sources that assert without showing may still be right, but you cannot tell,
which makes them useless for verification specifically. "According to experts"
is not evidence. "According to this dataset, collected this way" is.

## 2. It corrects itself in public

The single most informative property, and the most overlooked.

Everyone is wrong sometimes. A source that never appears to be wrong is not
more accurate than one that publishes corrections — it is one that does not
tell you. Visible corrections are the only observable evidence that a source
cares about accuracy rather than about appearing accurate.

Look for a corrections page, dated updates, an editor's note. Their absence
across a large body of work is a finding.

## 3. Its incentives are visible

The fastest single question in this module:

> **Who benefits if I believe this?**

Not a conspiracy test. Everyone has incentives, including good sources. The
question is whether they are *visible*, and whether the claim happens to be
exactly what the incentive would predict.

A vendor's study finding their category effective. An industry body finding
regulation harmful. A campaign group finding the problem urgent. None of these
are automatically wrong — and all of them need a second source before you rely
on them.

Undisclosed funding is worse than obvious funding. A visible interest can be
priced in; a hidden one cannot.

## 4. It separates knowledge from inference

Reliable sources are audibly careful about what they actually established
versus what they suspect. *We found X. This may suggest Y. We did not test Z.*

Sources that present inference in the same register as measurement are telling
you they do not track the difference — which means you cannot track it either.

This is the same property E4·L3 asked summaries to preserve, and E5·L7 warned
about losing. It runs through the whole curriculum because it is where accuracy
mostly lives.

## Authority is a weak signal, not no signal

A well-regarded institution is somewhat more likely to be right, and it is much
weaker evidence than people treat it as. Institutions have been wrong at length,
have incentives, and are cited for things they never said.

Use authority to decide **where to look first**. Do not use it to decide what
is true. That is exactly the E7 pattern: signals point, evidence decides.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Take a source you rely on. Check whether it has a corrections page, whether it
shows its evidence, and who funds it.

Then ask who benefits if you believe its main claim. If the answer is "the
people who wrote it", find a second source before using it.

## Recap

Judge a source by whether it shows its evidence, corrects itself in public,
makes its incentives visible, and distinguishes what it found from what it
infers. Ask who benefits if you believe it. Treat authority as a hint about
where to look, never as a substitute for looking.
