Everything so far has treated verification as a technical problem: find the
source, check the claim, judge the site. Those are the easy part.

The hard part is that you are not a neutral instrument, and an assistant that
produces plausible support for almost any position is unusually good at
supplying whatever you were already inclined to believe.

## Asymmetric scrutiny

The core mechanism, and it is not exotic.

When an answer contradicts what you think, you check it. You notice the hedge,
wonder about the source, ask a follow-up.

When an answer agrees with you, you move on.

Both feel like normal reading. Neither feels like bias. But you have applied
two completely different standards to two claims of identical reliability, and
the one that survived unexamined is the one that matched your prior.

E7·L2 named this trigger — an unusually convenient answer deserves a check —
and this lesson is why: the risk lives in your reaction, not in the model.

## Why knowing this changes nothing

Worth being blunt. Reading about confirmation bias does not reduce it. It
operates before you notice, and the feeling of being fair-minded is unrelated
to whether you are being fair.

What helps is not awareness. It is **procedure** — something you do that does
not depend on catching yourself in the act.

## Three that actually work

**Decide what would change your mind, in advance.**

Before researching, write: *I would abandon this if I saw ___.* Specific and
falsifiable.

This works because you set the standard before you knew which way the evidence
went. Afterwards, the standard moves — you will find reasons why the evidence
you found is not quite the evidence you meant.

**Ask for the case against, and take it seriously.**

E4·L4's technique, applied to yourself:

> *What is the strongest case against [my position]? Steel-man it. Do not
> balance it with counterarguments.*

The forbidding matters. Otherwise you get an objection immediately defused, and
you experience having considered it without having.

**Notice when you stopped looking.**

The moment you found a source confirming your position and felt satisfied — that
satisfaction is the signal. Ask whether you would have stopped there had it said
the opposite.

## Where an assistant makes it worse

Two specific ways, worth naming.

It is **agreeable**. Push back on a correct answer and it will often concede.
If you are motivated, you can walk almost any model to almost any conclusion,
and the transcript will read like inquiry.

It **supplies fluent support for anything**. Ask for the case for a position
and you will get a well-organised one — regardless of the evidence. Fluency
here is a function of the request, not of whether the position is sound.

Both mean an assistant can manufacture the *experience* of having investigated
something while confirming what you brought.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Take something you believe about your field that colleagues disagree with.

Write down what would change your mind. Then ask for the strongest case
against, with counterarguments forbidden.

Notice whether you are reading it to understand it, or to find the flaw. That
noticing is the skill.

## Recap

You scrutinise unwelcome answers and wave through welcome ones, and knowing
that does not fix it. Use procedure instead: write down what would change your
mind before you look, ask for the strongest case against with rebuttals
forbidden, and treat the feeling of satisfaction as the signal that you stopped
early. An agreeable assistant can manufacture the experience of inquiry.
