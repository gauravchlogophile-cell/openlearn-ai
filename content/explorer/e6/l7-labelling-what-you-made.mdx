You have made something. Does it need to say so?

"Always" is the easy answer and it is not right — nobody labels a stock
illustration on a slide. "Never" is obviously wrong. The line is more useful
than either.

## The test

> Would a reasonable person take this as a record of something that actually
> happened?

If yes, say what it is. If no, you are usually fine.

A generated illustration on a blog post: no label needed. It is decoration, and
nobody thinks it is a photograph of a real event.

A photorealistic image of a named place, a real person, or a specific
occurrence: label it. That is the case where the medium's implicit promise —
*this happened, and someone was there* — is being borrowed.

This is why L5 flagged video specifically. Video carries that promise far more
strongly than a still.

## Four places the answer is always yes

Regardless of the test:

- **News, journalism, documentary.** Non-negotiable.
- **Anything involving a real identifiable person**, including their voice.
- **Evidence** — insurance, legal, workplace complaints, any record intended to
  establish what occurred.
- **Where a rule already applies.** Advertising standards, platform policies,
  your employer, and in a growing number of jurisdictions, the law. The EU AI
  Act's transparency obligations are the most cited example; more are arriving.

## Provenance metadata, honestly

There is a standard, **C2PA**, sometimes branded Content Credentials. It attaches
signed information to a file — what made it, when, what happened to it since —
and support is growing across cameras, editors and generation tools.

It is worth using where your tools offer it. Two things it does not do, and
both are commonly misunderstood.

**It cannot prove an untagged file is authentic.** Absence of provenance is not
evidence of anything, because metadata is stripped constantly by ordinary
activity: uploading to most platforms, taking a screenshot, resizing, sending
through a messaging app. Most honest media in circulation has no credentials
attached.

**It is not a detector.** It travels with files that carry it. It says nothing
about the ones that do not.

So treat it as a way of **signing your own work**, not as a way of checking
other people's. E7 covers verification properly.

## Label it visibly, not just in metadata

Following from that: if disclosure matters, put it where a person will see it —
a caption, a line in the credits, text in the corner.

Metadata that survives your export and dies on upload has disclosed nothing to
anyone. A visible caption survives a screenshot.

## The cost is almost nothing

The strongest argument for disclosing is not ethical, it is practical.

The cost of labelling is a line of text. The cost of *not* labelling, and being
found out, is that everything else you have published becomes questionable —
including the parts that were real. That asymmetry is not close, and it does not
require you to have settled any philosophical question.

## Try it now (5 minutes)

Take three pieces of generated media you have made or seen. Apply the test to
each.

For any that need a label, write the actual caption — the sentence you would
use. It is shorter than you expect.

## Recap

Label anything a reasonable person would take as a record of what happened, and
always for news, real people, evidence, or where a rule applies. Use provenance
metadata to sign your own work, knowing it cannot authenticate anyone else's,
and put the disclosure somewhere a person will actually see it.
