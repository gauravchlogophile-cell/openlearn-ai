Synthetic speech has become genuinely good, and much of what it enables is
straightforwardly positive. It also contains the sharpest ethical line in this
module, and the line is easy to state.

## Two different things

**Synthetic voice** is a voice belonging to nobody — built to sound human,
matching no particular person. Use it freely. It is what narrates a video,
reads a document aloud, or gives a product an audio interface.

**Voice cloning** reproduces a *specific, identifiable* person from samples of
their speech. This is a different act, and it requires their permission.

The distinction is not about the technology, which is similar. It is about
whether there is a person on the other end of it.

## Where synthetic voice earns its place

Worth stating plainly, because the cloning conversation tends to swallow the
whole subject.

**Accessibility.** People who cannot read a screen, or read comfortably, or at
all. Lrnon has read-aloud for exactly this reason.

**Narration** for material that would otherwise have none, because studio time
was never in the budget.

**Multiple languages** for material that would otherwise reach one.

**Voice restoration** for people losing the ability to speak — banked from
their own recordings, with their consent, and one of the clearest goods in the
whole field.

None of these need anyone else's voice.

## The consent standard

If a voice is identifiably a real person's, you need that person's permission.
Not implied, not assumed, not inferred from a public recording existing.

**Explicit** — they said yes.
**Informed** — they know it is a synthetic reproduction, not a recording.
**Specific** — for this use. Permission to narrate a training video is not
permission to make them endorse a product.

A recording being public does not grant this. A podcast, a lecture, an
interview — all publicly available, none of them consent to be synthesised.

And note that impersonating a real person can be unlawful quite apart from
ethics: fraud, defamation, and in a growing number of jurisdictions specific
likeness and synthetic-media provisions. "It was just a demo" is not a defence
once it is distributed.

## Your own voice, and the family case

Cloning your own voice is fine, and useful — narrate once, correct typos in
text rather than re-recording.

Two cautions worth having in advance. A clone of your voice is a credential
someone else could misuse, so treat the samples and the account like
credentials. And **voice authentication** systems that identify you by voice are
weakened by a good clone existing. Prefer another factor where your bank offers
one.

The case that comes up most often, and deserves a real answer: recreating the
voice of someone who has died, from old recordings. There is no clean rule. It
can be tender and it can be distressing, sometimes for different people in the
same family. The person cannot consent, so the question is who is being served —
and if the answer is anyone other than the people grieving, it is the wrong
project. Ask them first, and accept a no.

## Try it now (6 minutes)

Generate a short narration with a synthetic voice — a paragraph of your own
writing.

Then write down, in one sentence, the rule you will apply before ever using a
real person's voice. Having decided in advance is the whole point.

## Recap

Synthetic voice belongs to nobody and is genuinely valuable, especially for
accessibility. Cloning a real person needs explicit, informed, specific consent,
which a public recording does not supply — and impersonation can be unlawful
regardless. Your own voice is yours to clone, with the caution that it is also a
credential.
